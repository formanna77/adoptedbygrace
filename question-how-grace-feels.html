Why Does Grace Feel Like a Choice?

Peter's yes was real. It was also not the thing that held him.

March 30, 2026 Questions

He prayed for a man who was hours from denying him, and the word He used was when, not if.

In Brief: Your conversion felt like a decision because it was one. What it could not do was report its own cause — the sensation of authorship is not a readout of who moved first. Scripture settles this with a transcript rather than a theory. Jesus predicted Peter's collapse and his return before either happened, and Peter lived both from the inside as his own freest acts. The word for that is compatibilism; the charcoal fire on the beach is the proof.

Ask a believer how he came to Christ, and listen to the grammar of the answer. I decided. I gave my life to Him. I chose. However it happened—a crowded room or a quiet kitchen, one hour or fifteen slow years—the memory keeps the same shape. At some point you turned, and it felt like the most deliberate thing you had ever done.

Then you read Ephesians 2:4-5. God, "because of his great love for us," "made us alive with Christ even when we were dead in transgressions." The sentence has God for its subject and you for its object, and the operation is performed on a corpse. Nothing in it is offered. It is done.

Two accounts of the same morning, and neither one will get up from the table.

The usual question here is did I choose, or did God choose for me, and it has kept better minds than ours busy for centuries. But it is the wrong question, and the right one is narrower and less comfortable: which part of that memory were you ever entitled to trust? You have a vivid record of what the turning felt like. You have never had access to what caused it. You are reading a sensation as though it were a report, and it was never a report. We are the only creatures who mistake the sense of authorship for evidence of it, and we do it most confidently about the things we did least.

Scripture does not answer this with a theory of the will. It answers with a transcript. There is one man in the Bible whose free decisions were predicted out loud before he made them, in the same room, in front of witnesses—and whose own account of living through them survives. Everything you want to know is in his file.

Two Promises Were Made in That Room

On the last night, at the table, Jesus turns to Peter.

"Simon, Simon, Satan has asked to sift all of you as wheat. But I have prayed for you, Simon, that your faith may not fail. And when you have turned back, strengthen your brothers."

Luke 22:31-32

Peter answers immediately, and it is the best sentence of his life so far: "Lord, I am ready to go with you to prison and to death."

He was not posturing. Look at the rest of his week—he draws a sword on an armed detachment in a garden, untrained and hopelessly outnumbered, and swings at a man's head. Whatever else Peter was, he was not a coward, and he was not lying at that table. He had examined himself and reported accurately on what he found there. He wanted to die with Jesus. The desire was real, present, and his own.

And Jesus, having just heard the best sentence of Peter's life, answered him: "I tell you, Peter, before the rooster crows today, you will deny three times that you know me."

Two promises are now on the record in that room, made minutes apart. One of them is going to hold.

The Fire He Was Warming His Hands At

Six hours later Peter is in a courtyard, and John—who was standing there—records a detail that looks like set dressing and is not.

It was cold, and the servants and officials stood around a fire they had made to keep warm. Peter also was standing with them, warming himself.

John 18:18

Hold on to that fire. John is going to need it again.

Three questions come at him out of the dark—a servant girl, a bystander, a relative of the man whose ear he had cut off—and three times the mouth that promised prison and death says it does not know him. Then a rooster. Then, in Luke's account, one line: "The Lord turned and looked straight at Peter." He went outside and wept bitterly.

Now set the two scenes side by side, because this is the whole matter. At the table, Peter's will was pointed at martyrdom, he knew it, and he was right about it. In the courtyard, Peter's will was pointed at self-preservation, and he never saw it coming. Same man, same night, sincere both times. His introspection was accurate as a description of what he wanted in the moment and worthless as a prediction of what he would do. The heart is deceitful above all things—and Jeremiah was not talking about lying to other people.

The Word That Was Not "If"

Go back to the table and read what Jesus said about the aftermath: "And when you have turned back, strengthen your brothers."

Not if. There is no conditional particle in that clause—no ean, the ordinary Greek word for "if"—and the participle Luke uses, epistrepsas, is an aorist: the turning is handled as an accomplished event, something to be looked back on, and the instruction is issued on the far side of it. That is why the NIV renders it "when," and why every serious English translation does the same. Jesus is not voicing a hope about Peter. He is telling a man what his own future free act is going to be, and then handing him a job to do once he has performed it.

Sit with the order of events. The prayer—"I have prayed for you, that your faith may not fail"—was offered before the failure it answered. The restoration was announced before the fall it would repair. And when Peter finally turns back, on a beach, he will live it as the most voluntary act of his life: his own grief, his own legs, his own answers, wrung out of him and no one else.

He will be right about all of that. It will be his. And it was on the record before he did it.

This is what the theologians mean by compatibilism, with the word taken away. Your freedom is not a courtesy God extends around the edges of His plan. It is one of the things His plan does. A free act and a decreed act are not two acts, which is why grace can be irresistible without anyone being dragged.

Where Your Willing Comes From

Peter's file answers the phenomenology. It does not yet name the mechanism. Paul does that.

Therefore, my dear friends, as you have always obeyed—not only in my presence, but now much more in my absence—continue to work out your salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you to will and to act in order to fulfill his good purpose.

Philippians 2:12-13

Read the English at speed and you can still hear cooperation in it: God supplies the power, you supply the willing, the two meet somewhere in the middle. The Greek closes that door. Paul writes ho energōn en hymin kai to thelein kai to energein—"the one energizing in you"—and that participle takes two objects, the first of which is to thelein: the willing itself. God is not energizing you so that you may then produce a willing. The willing is what is being produced. And Paul reaches for the same root twice, for God's working and for your acting, inside one clause—a deliberate collapse of the distance the NIV keeps in sense and loses in sound.

Test it where you sit. Take something you do not want—early rising, the neighbor you avoid, a chapter of Leviticus—and want it. Not do it. Want it, now, on command. Nothing happens; and notice more precisely where nothing happens. There is no muscle to tense, no place behind your eyes to push from, no lever anywhere in the room. You have no organ for this. Wanting is not among the things a person can decide to do—it is what all your deciding is downstream of. Which means the arrival of a new wanting is not an achievement. Wherever one appears, it was put there, and the heart that now has it was replaced.

You will find that you already live this way in every other room of your life. Nobody selected his mother tongue, and no one composing a sentence in it feels dictated to.

The Yes You Cannot Manufacture

Underneath the argument about mechanism there is nearly always a different question, and it is the one actually worth answering: if He made me want Him, was it ever really love?

You know the answer, because you have never once applied that standard anywhere else. Nobody decides to fall in love. It is discovered, usually late, and usually by somebody else first. No man has ever concluded that his marriage was counterfeit on the grounds that he failed to legislate his own affection into being, or that his mother's love is suspect because she did not pick it off a menu. A love that had to be self-generated to count would be the only kind of love no human being has ever had. Everywhere else we accept without argument that the deepest loves are the ones that happened to us. Then we reach God and make an exception—demanding that this one love be the unassisted product of our own will, or we will not call it ours.

That exception is not humility. It is the last position the self is still holding.

And it costs the holder something. A love you generated is a love you have to keep generating. If the warmth is the ground, the ground moves every morning—and on the mornings when you feel nothing at all, the flu mornings, the grief mornings, the flat ones with no cause you can name, you will read the emptiness as a verdict on yourself. Peter had that problem in its acute form. He had once put his own sincerity up as collateral and watched it default in a courtyard, by a fire.

He Chose Charcoal

Weeks later. Galilee, early morning. Seven of them have fished all night and caught nothing, and a man on the shore tells them where to put the net. When they reach land, John records what is already waiting.

When they landed, they saw a fire of burning coals there with fish on it, and some bread.

John 21:9

The NIV gives you "a fire of burning coals" here, and back in the courtyard only "a fire." In Greek they are the same word: anthrakia, a charcoal fire. It occurs twice in the entire New Testament, both times in John—once in the courtyard where Peter denied him, once on the beach where Jesus made him breakfast. The NIV's own cross-reference at John 21:9 sends the reader straight back to John 18:18. The editors saw it. The English just cannot carry it.

Christ reached that shore before the boat did, and He built a fire, and He built it out of charcoal. Peter walked up a beach into a smell he had last breathed on the worst night of his life. Then, over those coals, three questions—do you love me, do you love me, do you love me—one laid over each denial like new flooring.

And watch what Peter has learned. He does not say yes, and this time I mean it. He does not offer his sincerity again; he has seen the exchange rate on that. By the third question he has stopped testifying about himself altogether: "Lord, you know all things; you know that I love you." The evidence has been moved out of Peter and into Christ. He is no longer the witness. He is the exhibit.

Which is where the question you arrived with finally goes. You are not being asked to rule on whether your turning was genuine; you would be an unreliable court, and Peter's file is the proof of it. You are being asked to notice who was praying before you fell, who was standing on the shore before you landed, and who lit the fire. You did not choose Him, and the choosing happened somewhere you have never been—which is precisely why nothing you do tomorrow can unmake it.

Your choosing was real. Peter's were too, both of them, the brave one and the cowardly one. What held was never on that side of the ledger. What held was a prayer said in advance for a man who was about to fail, by someone who already knew he would, and who used the word when.

There is one more line in that chapter, and it is doing more work than it looks like. Jesus, standing over the coals with breakfast already made, says: "Come and have breakfast."

He was cooking before you got there. He had already lit the fire.
