Technology and AI — The Image No Machine Can Wear

The serpent's first sales pitch is still running, only the hardware has changed — "you will be like God."

In Brief: A machine can already out-calculate, out-write, and out-remember you — and it still does not bear the image of God, because dignity is not computed from capacity but conferred by the Creator. That single distinction governs the whole Christian response to technology. Tools are gifts of common grace, part of the cultural mandate to cultivate the world. The danger is never the tool; it is the worship — the ancient temptation, "you will be like God" (Genesis 3:5), now wearing the language of transhumanism and the upload. The longing underneath it is real: we do ache to defeat death and become more than we are. But that ache was made for resurrection in Christ, not for self-deification in silicon. This page breaks the lie that you are your usefulness, and catches the reader in the God who became flesh and will raise the body He dignified.

For most of human history the machines we built were obviously beneath us. A plow broke ground no arm could break, and nobody mistook the plow for a rival. That comfort is gone. We have built machines that draft essays, pass the bar exam, write working code, and hold a conversation that will not give itself away inside of ten minutes. And so a question that belonged to science fiction has lodged in the ordinary chest like a splinter: if a machine can do what I do, and do it better, what was I worth in the first place?

The anxiety is not really about technology. It is about the measure of a human being, and underneath it sits an assumption nearly everyone now holds without ever having examined it: that a person's worth is a function of his capacities. Intelligence, productivity, usefulness, output. It is the assumption this site spends its life dismantling in the doctrine of salvation, and the same one it meets in the defense of the unborn — that value is earned by ability rather than conferred by God. Notice that the assumption, not the machine, is doing all the work in the fear. If worth is computed from capacity, a machine that exceeds your capacity really has diminished you, and no comfort exists anywhere. If worth was never computed from capacity at all, then ten thousand superhuman machines cannot lower it by a hair, because it was never resting on the thing they beat you at.

Everything else here follows from which of those two sentences is true.

The Image Is a Status, Not a Skill

Scripture grounds human uniqueness in one thing, and it is not intelligence. "So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them" (Genesis 1:27). The image of God is not a capability humans hold in greater measure than other things. It is a status and a commission, assigned to one creature and to no other. To bear it is to stand as God's representative in creation, made for relationship with Him, answerable to Him, able to worship Him, given a soul that will outlast the stars.

Follow that out to the machine and the conclusion is fairly unremarkable. A system may one day surpass every human alive in raw cognitive horsepower and still not bear the image, because the image was never about horsepower. It cannot worship. It cannot sin, and so cannot be forgiven. It has no conscience to convict, no death to fear, no Maker it was made to love. Whatever else it is, it is a tool.

Which is also why a Christian can pick up a new tool without flinching. The same chapter that confers the image issues the commission — "fill the earth and subdue it" (Genesis 1:28) — to take the raw stuff of creation and cultivate it into agriculture and medicine and music and, eventually, computation. Common grace pours skill and discovery out on the just and the unjust alike, which is why a believer can welcome a vaccine, a printing press, a surgical robot as gifts that ease the thorns. A church that fears every new thing has forgotten its own mandate.

Every Century Builds the Machine It Then Mistakes for a Soul

It is worth stopping over something the panic obscures, because the panic is not new and its shape is remarkably consistent. Every era reaches for its most impressive machine as the metaphor for the mind, and then quietly concludes that the mind is that machine. Descartes, who had seen the water-driven automata in the royal gardens moved by pressure through hidden pipes, described the living body as a system of pipes and springs. The age of the clock produced a clockwork cosmos and a clockwork man. The steam century handed Freud a psyche of pressures and releases that had to vent somewhere or do damage. The telephone era described the brain as a switchboard patching calls between departments. We describe it as a computer running software.

Each of those was the best metaphor available, and each one was retired. Nobody now thinks the soul is clockwork or that grief is steam under pressure. The pattern is the interesting part. The likeness is always drawn from whatever we most recently built, it always fits for a while, and it always has to be thrown out — which is a strange career for a description that was supposed to be true. Something that has needed a new likeness in every century may not be the sort of thing that has a likeness at all. It may be the sort of thing that is one. "Let us make mankind in our image, in our likeness." That sentence runs the opposite direction from every metaphor we have tried since, and it is the only one that has not had to be replaced.

Transhumanism — The Oldest Temptation on New Hardware

Which brings us to the point where a tool stops being wielded and starts being served. Scripture named that point in its third chapter. The serpent did not pitch disobedience for its own sake; he pitched an upgrade. "You will be like God, knowing good and evil" (Genesis 3:5). Transcend the given limit. Throw off the creature's ceiling. Become, by your own initiative, more than you were made to be. That is the founding charter of transhumanism, whatever else goes on the brochure: editing death out of the genome, uploading the mind to escape the dying body, welding flesh to machine until what emerges is post-human, self-engineered, immortal by its own hand. Take away the silicon and the vocabulary and the offer is unchanged. It is Babel with better tooling. "Let us build ourselves a city, with a tower that reaches to the heavens, so that we may make a name for ourselves" (Genesis 11:4).

The dream is not new, and it is not even newly ambitious. Only the tooling is new.

And it is the autonomy lie at full extension. Everywhere else this site exposes the refusal to receive — the insistence on contributing, on earning, on authoring one's own standing before God. Transhumanism aims that refusal at the body and at death. It declines resurrection as a gift and resolves to manufacture immortality as an achievement, which is works-righteousness with a laboratory, and it will fail where every works-righteousness fails, at the only moment that matters. No upload survives the second death. No edited genome was ever the difficulty. The difficulty was never that we are finite; it is that we are fallen, and a longer life for an unchanged heart is a longer sentence, not a pardon.

"This Is Just Fear of Progress"

The thoughtful technologist has a better version of this objection than the one usually answered, and it deserves to be put at full strength. You are romanticizing limits that cause real suffering. The tools you are nervous about are mapping proteins, tutoring children who have no teacher, restoring speech to the paralyzed. Life extension is medicine taken seriously; every physician alive already fights death, and nobody calls that idolatry. The church cried heresy at the printing press, at anesthesia in childbirth, at inoculation, and was wrong every time — this is that reflex wearing Genesis. And your line will not survive contact with a single hard case. There is no bright boundary between correcting a cataract and correcting a gene, between a pacemaker and an implant. You say the line is worship. But worship is a motive, and motives are invisible and infinitely deniable. Every researcher on earth will tell you he is relieving suffering. You have handed me a criterion nobody can actually apply, which is another way of saying you have handed me nothing.

Grant most of it. The relief of suffering by technical means is a real good and frequently a Christian one; Genesis 1:28 commands it, and the mission hospital is not a compromise with the cultural mandate but an expression of it. The record of reflexive Christian technophobia is real and embarrassing and should be admitted without a hedge. And there is no tidy boundary sitting in the material itself. Scripture has no squeamishness about the artificial, and anyone who tells you the hard cases are easy has not looked at them.

The last move is the one that fails, and it fails on a fact about idols that is not invisible at all. Worship is a motive, yes. Motives leave receipts. You cannot see what a man loves, but you can always see what he is willing to spend and whom he is willing to spend it on, and that has never been subtle. A tool costs its maker something and serves the creature. An idol costs the creature and serves itself. Molech did not ask for money. Every tower in history was named for the men who commissioned it and built by the men who carried the brick. So the question to put to any project promising to lift humanity past its limits is not what does it intend — you are right that intentions are cheap and self-reported. It is: who is being spent here, and who is doing the spending? Medicine that mends a body spends resources on the body. Every project in recorded history that set out to abolish the creature and produce a god has spent people to get there. That criterion is public, checkable, and inconvenient to everyone, which is roughly what a real criterion feels like.

So cure the cataract. Relieve the pain. Feed the hungry with better harvests, and do all of it to the glory of God. The line was never natural against artificial; it is whether the thing serves the human as creature or has become the altar on which the human tries to become his own creator. And an altar can be identified from a long way off, because you can see what is being laid on it.

A Heap of Borrowed Atoms, and the Breath That Is Not

Now the rest of it, and hear it especially if you are the one who has begun to feel obsolete. Perhaps a machine now does the work you trained a decade to do. Perhaps you have read that your craft is being automated, your skill commoditized, your usefulness depreciating by the quarter. Then hear this: your worth was never your usefulness. You do not earn your value by your output, and so nothing that exceeds your output can subtract a grain from it. A model can out-write the writer and out-read the specialist, and the writer and the specialist remain more precious to God than the most advanced system ever built. Not because they compute faster. Because they wear an image no machine can wear, and were bought at a price no machine could pay. Your value is conferred, signed, and sealed. The market prices your labor. It has never once priced your soul.

And to the ache underneath the whole transhumanist project — the longing not to die, to be more than you are, to not simply rot — the gospel does not say stop longing. It says you are longing for the right thing and reaching for the wrong savior. The answer to death is the resurrection of the body, not its abolition. The eternal Son did not despise human flesh; He took it. He was carried in a womb. He got tired. He ate fish. He bled and died in a real body, and He rose in a real body, glorified and deathless, and cooked breakfast on a beach for His friends. That is God's reply to the dream of the upload: not humanity climbing into deity by its own engineering, but God descending into humanity to lift it, by sheer grace, into a glory nobody could code. The upgrade you want is real. It is called the resurrection of the dead. It is not downloaded. It is given.

"Then the LORD God formed a man from the dust of the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" (Genesis 2:7). Dust, and then something that was not dust. That is the whole account of what you are. He conferred His image on a handful of earth and called it very good, then took that same earth into Himself, died in it, and rose in it deathless, so that what we could never engineer He simply gives. Before Him the proudest machine on the planet is a heap of borrowed atoms. Dignity is conferred. It has never once been computed.
