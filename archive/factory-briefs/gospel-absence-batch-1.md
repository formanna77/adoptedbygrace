<p>
  On sovereignty: if God's choice of worlds is constrained by brute-fact counterfactuals, His sovereignty collapses into a cosmic version of "doing the best He can with the options available." It is logically possible, on Molinist terms, that no feasible world exists in which all God's purposes are accomplished — that the counterfactuals happen to be unfriendly to His aims. God's plan becomes contingent on creaturely cooperation He did not author. That is not the God of Isaiah 46. That is a God fenced in by the hypothetical refusals of hypothetical creatures.
</p>
   <p>
  On freedom: if God creates a person knowing with absolute certainty what they will do in every circumstance, and places them in circumstances chosen precisely to produce the outcome He wanted — in what meaningful sense does the creature possess the power of contrary choice? The creature cannot do otherwise than what God foreknew and arranged. The libertarian freedom Molinism was built to preserve evaporates under the weight of its own certainty. What remains is either soft <a href="/secular-philosophy-free-will">determinism</a> (which is compatibilism under a different nameplate) or genuine randomness (which is not freedom at all).
</p>
   <p>
  Molinism was built to preserve two things at once. It preserves neither.
</p>
</div>
</div>
</section>

<!-- SCRIPTURE -->
<section class="section-block" id="scripture">
    <h2>What Does Scripture Actually Teach?</h2>

    <div class="scripture-block">
  "Our God is in heaven; he does whatever pleases him."
   <span class="scripture-ref">— Psalm 115:3</span>
</div>

    <p>
  Scripture does not present a God who selects among available options constrained by creaturely freedom. It presents a God who <strong><em>"works out everything in conformity with the purpose of his will"</em></strong> (Ephesians 1:11) — not in conformity with counterfactuals He did not author, not in conformity with a menu handed down from metaphysical nowhere.
</p>

    <div class="scripture-block">
  "I am God, and there is no other; I am God, and there is none like me. I make known the end from the beginning, from ancient times, what is still to come. I say: My purpose will stand, and I will do all that I please."
   <span class="scripture-ref">— Isaiah 46:9–10</span>
</div>

    <p>
  God does not merely <em>foresee</em> the end from the beginning. He <em>declares</em> it. His purpose <em>will stand</em>. He <em>will do</em> all that He pleases. That is the grammar of causal sovereignty, not the grammar of world-selection from a menu of possibilities. The verbs are transitive. The subject is God. The objects are the ends of history, named in advance, because they were decreed in advance.
</p>

    <p>
  The key passage Molinists appeal to is <strong>1 Samuel 23:10–13</strong>, where David asks God what Saul would do if David stayed in Keilah, and God tells him. Molinists argue this demonstrates counterfactual knowledge of free choices. The Reformed response is straightforward: God knows counterfactuals because He knows His own decree. He knows what He <em>would have decreed</em> in those circumstances — what He <em>would have sovereignly brought about</em> in that hypothetical. No "middle" moment of knowledge, independent of His will, is required. The passage proves omniscience. It does not prove Molinism.
</p>
</section>

<!-- VERDICT -->
<section class="section-block" id="verdict">
    <div class="verdict">
   <div class="verdict-label">Biblical Verdict</div>
   <p>
  Molinism is an ingenious philosophical construction — and a solution to a problem Scripture does not recognize. The Bible never strains to reconcile sovereignty with libertarian freedom, because it never affirms libertarian freedom. It affirms that God determines all things (Ephesians 1:11; Isaiah 46:10; Proverbs 16:33) and that humans make real, meaningful, morally accountable choices (Genesis 50:20; Acts 2:23; Acts 4:27–28). This is compatibilism. It needs no middle knowledge to hold it together. It needs only the God who is.
</p>
   <p>
  The heart of the matter is this. Molinism wants a God sovereign <em>without</em> being the ultimate cause. Scripture presents a God sovereign <em>because</em> He is the ultimate cause.
</p>
   <p style="padding: 1.2rem 1.5rem; border-left: 3px solid var(--accent-ember); margin: 1.5rem 0; background: rgba(232,100,44,0.05);">
  "For from him and through him and for him are all things. To him be the glory forever! Amen." — Romans 11:36
</p>
   <p>
  Not some things. Not the circumstances around things. Not the menu from which things were selected.
</p>
   <p>
   <strong><em>All things.</em></strong>
</p>

   <p style="margin-top: 1.5rem;">
  The Reformed position does not diminish <a href="/objection-responsibility">human responsibility</a> — it grounds it in divine sovereignty. The creature acts. The creature is accountable. And above the creature's acting, behind it, beneath it, through it, stands the God whose purpose stood before the stars were hung, stands now as you read this sentence, and will stand when the last page of history is read aloud in the courts of heaven. His plan was not selected from a catalog of feasible worlds. His plan was <em>spoken</em> — and the speaking was the making — and the making was the world in which you were chosen before its foundation was laid.
</p>

   <p>
  If you are His, you are not His because a hypothetical version of you in a hypothetical world freely chose Him under circumstances He happened to find convenient. You are His because He said your name before He said <em>let there be light</em>, purchased you before you drew breath, and will keep you by the same sovereign word that made the stars stand still in their sockets. The word that called the worlds into being is the word that calls you home.
</p>
