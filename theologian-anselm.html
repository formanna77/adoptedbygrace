Anselm of Canterbury

The medieval architect who proved God's absolute supremacy through reason and prayer.

In Brief: Anselm set out to prove God by reason alone, with Christ deliberately removed from the argument — and got further than anyone before or since. Then, eleven chapters into the book that made him famous, he came to the one thing his reasoning could not move: why God saves these and not those, among men equally guilty. He wrote it down, said reason ends here, and went back to praying. Four hundred years before Luther, the sharpest mind of the Middle Ages reasoned his way to the bottom and found sovereign mercy holding up the floor.

Everyone who has ever doubted has run the same experiment in private: if I could think it through far enough, I would know. Most of us abandon it somewhere around two in the afternoon on a bad Tuesday. Anselm of Canterbury ran it in public, on paper, for fifty years, with more mind than the rest of us will be issued.

He is remembered for what happened at the top of that project — one idea, turned over in the dark, that philosophy departments have spent nine centuries failing to file away. But the famous argument is not the interesting thing about him, and he would have said so first. The interesting thing is where the reasoning finally stopped. It did not stop at the existence of God. It did not stop at the Trinity or the necessity of the cross. It stopped at one question, and he wrote the surrender down in his own hand, and almost nobody quotes it.

The Man Who Removed Christ From the Argument

The externals are quickly told. Born in 1033 at Aosta, high in the Alps. Left home in his twenties, wandered Burgundy and France, and entered the abbey of Bec in Normandy around 1060, drawn by the reputation of its prior, Lanfranc. Prior himself by 1063, abbot by 1078, archbishop of Canterbury by 1093. Two English kings wanted the Church's lands and revenues; Anselm would not sign them over; he was exiled twice and spent something close to a third of his archbishopric outside the country. He finished his greatest book in 1098 while banished from his own cathedral. He died at Canterbury on 21 April 1109.

The method is the part worth slowing down for. His monks asked him for a meditation on God that leaned on no authority at all — no Scripture cited, no father quoted, nothing but argument — and he gave them the Monologion, written sola ratione, by reason alone. In Cur Deus Homo he went further and worked remoto Christo: "with Christ removed," reasoning about the incarnation as though he had never heard that it happened.

That is not what modern apologetics does when it brackets the Bible. Modern apologetics brackets it to be persuasive — to meet the outsider on ground he already accepts. Anselm bracketed it to find out whether the thing was true all the way down. He was not building a ramp for anybody. He was on his hands and knees, testing the floor under his own feet, in a building he had already decided to live in.

The Sentence He Could Not Put Down

The preface to the Proslogion tells the story with a candor almost no philosopher permits himself. He had begun hunting unum argumentum — one single argument, needing nothing but itself, that would establish everything at once. He could not find it. He gave up. He tried to put the whole question out of his head so it would stop wasting his attention. And then, he writes, it came at him harder: magis ac magis nolenti et defendenti se — more and more, to a man unwilling, to a man defending himself against it.

Read that grammar again, because Anselm is doing something in it that he never comments on. Nolenti and defendenti are datives. They are not the subject of the sentence. They are the person the sentence is happening to. The most famous argument for the existence of God in the history of thought arrives in its author's own account the way grace arrives everywhere else in Scripture: unbidden, against resistance, to someone with his hands up. He did not go and get it. It came and got him.

The argument, stated fairly and briefly. Take the phrase something than which nothing greater can be thought. Anyone who hears it understands it — that is all it takes to be in his mind. But a thing that exists only in the mind is not as great as the same thing existing in fact. So if that than which nothing greater can be thought existed only in the mind, you could think of something greater — namely, it, existing. Which makes it not what it was defined as. So it exists.

Here is the Latin, and it repays a slow look:

Aliquid quo nihil maius cogitari potest. Something than which nothing greater can be thought.

Anselm writes that formula two ways in the same chapter, and the difference is not decoration. When he is praying — we believe you to be something than which nothing greater can be thought — he writes possit, subjunctive, the mood of a thing held in belief. When the formula stands on its own, as the plain content the fool takes in the moment he hears the words, he writes potest, indicative, the mood of a fact. The grammar quietly concedes the whole point of the book. To the man on his knees it is confessed. To the man denying it, it is simply the case.

And the fool is load-bearing, which is the part that gets lost. Anselm's argument does not run through the believer's mind at all. It runs through the unbeliever's: cum audit, intelligit quod audit — when he hears it, he understands what he hears, and what is understood is in the understanding. The man who says there is no God cannot so much as take in the sentence without the thing being in him. Paul says it flatly and without the syllogism: God's "invisible qualities—his eternal power and divine nature—have been clearly seen, being understood from what has been made, so that people are without excuse." Anselm has simply found the same excuse-lessness one layer further in, inside the equipment the denial is issued from.

Now the honesty this site owes you. The argument has been under fire since the week it was published, and much of the fire has been friendly. Gaunilo, a monk at Marmoutier, answered in Anselm's own lifetime with the lost-island objection, and Anselm answered him back. Thomas Aquinas rejected it outright two centuries later: in the Summa Theologiae he asks whether God's existence is self-evident and concludes that it is not self-evident to us, since we have no direct grasp of the divine essence — adding, drily, that not everyone who hears the word "God" understands "that than which nothing greater can be thought," seeing that some have believed God to be a body. Kant is usually credited with the burial. Alvin Plantinga exhumed a modal version of it in 1974 and it is arguing still.

So we stake nothing on it, and neither should you. What Anselm actually left the church is not the proof. It is the sentence at the end of his first chapter, which is the order of operations for every mind that has ever come to God: Neque enim quaero intelligere ut credam, sed credo ut intelligam.

"For I do not seek to understand in order that I may believe, but I believe in order that I may understand."

ANSELM, PROSLOGION

Which leaves the question his whole life turns on, and yours: where the believing itself came from.

Why God Could Not Simply Forgive

Twenty years later, in exile, he finished Cur Deus Homo — "Why God Became Man" — a dialogue with his student Boso, who asks the question every honest Christian eventually asks and mostly asks silently. Why could God not just forgive? He is God. Nobody could stop Him. Why the blood?

Anselm's answer works from a single idea: sin is not primarily a stain, it is a withholding. It is the refusal to render God what is owed Him. And the weight of an offense is measured by the one offended, not by the one offending — which is why the same shove is nothing in a crowd and treason in a throne room. A finite creature withholding from an infinite God incurs a debt with no ceiling on it.

God cannot simply waive that debt. Not because He lacks the power, but because to treat His own honor as though it were worth nothing would be to say something false about what He is worth — and He will not lie, least of all about Himself. So satisfaction has to be made. Satisfaction must come from man, because man is the one who owes it. Satisfaction can come only from God, because only God has anything infinite to give.

Deus homo. God-man. The title of the book is the answer to the book. And the whole architecture of the atonement stands or falls on that one hinge.

Sit with the shape of it before moving on, because everything else on this site is downstream of it. If the debt was of a size that required God Himself to become a man to discharge it, then nothing you carry is currency. Not your sincerity. Not your decision. Not your willingness to be reasonable about the whole thing. Those were never small payments toward a large bill; they were never on the ledger at all. This is monergism arrived at from the accounting side, by a man who had no Reformation vocabulary to reach for and did not need one.

A Debt Is Either Paid Or It Is Not

Now a correction, and it matters more than the point it corrects, because a case that has to shade its own sources is not a case.

Cur Deus Homo does not teach a definite atonement. Anselm never asks whose debt the payment discharged. When he weighs the worth of Christ's life he says it makes satisfaction "for the sins of the whole world" — and then immediately adds, "and infinitely more," which tells you he is measuring value and not scope. An infinite payment is infinite whether one debtor stands behind it or ten thousand. The question of extent was simply not on the table in 1098, and the century after him answered it the other way: Peter Lombard's Sentences, the standard theology textbook of the West for four hundred years, settled on the formula every medieval student memorized — Christ offered Himself for all as to the sufficiency of the price, for the elect only as to its efficacy.

So Anselm laid the powder. He did not strike the match.

It was John Owen, five hundred and fifty years later, who asked Anselm's own premise one question further, and the question is embarrassingly simple. If satisfaction was made for a man's sin, then that man's sin has been satisfied for. So either the payment covered every sin of every man — in which case no man can be charged for a debt already settled, and everyone is saved — or it covered the sins of some, and it saves them completely. What it cannot do is cover a sin and leave the sinner liable for it. That is not mercy with conditions attached. That is a receipt that does not work. (The full trilemma is here, and the objections are answered here.)

Owen did not smuggle a foreign doctrine into Anselm's satisfaction. He read the receipt.

The Freedom That Is Not the Power to Choose

Everyone knows what free will means. It means you could have done otherwise. Anselm looked at that definition and threw it out.

In De Libertate Arbitrii he points out something nobody wants to hear: if freedom is the power to sin or not sin, then God is not free, and neither are the angels who cannot fall, and neither will you be in glory. But those are the freest wills there are. So the power to sin cannot be a component of freedom. It is a defect in it. Nothing is freer than a will that has become incapable of falling.

What is freedom, then? Potestas servandi rectitudinem voluntatis propter ipsam rectitudinem — the power to keep the will's own rightness, for the sake of that rightness itself. Not the power to pick. The power to hold.

And here is the consequence Anselm draws himself, in the driest possible voice. A will that has this rightness can keep it. A will that has lost it cannot get it back. The machinery is untouched — you go on choosing, freely, dozens of times an hour, nobody forcing your hand. What is gone is the thing worth choosing. You are perfectly free to pursue whatever you love. You have no power whatsoever to love something else on command.

Test it rather than agreeing with it, because agreement costs nothing. Do not resolve to want God more than you want the next thing your hand is already reaching for. Just want it — right now, by deciding to. Notice what happens, or rather what does not. The will did not disobey you. It simply had nothing to work with. That failure, in the ten seconds it takes, is the doctrine of total depravity demonstrated on your own equipment, and it is why the New Testament speaks of the natural man as needing a new heart rather than better information.

Which is why Calvin, five centuries downstream, is doing repair work and not invention. The whole Reformed doctrine of the will is Anselm's distinction with the volume turned up: grace does not overpower what you want. It replaces it.

Where His Reason Stopped

Return to the Proslogion, chapter eleven, and to the sentence this whole page has been walking toward.

He has spent three chapters on the hardest thing in his book — not God's existence, which he considers settled, but how a wholly just God can spare the wicked at all. He solves it, and elegantly: God spares the wicked because it is just for God to be so good that no better could be conceived. Mercy is not a suspension of justice; it is justice done toward Himself. Nine words later he adds, almost as an aside: id solum iustum est quod vis, et non iustum quod non vis — that alone is just which You will, and not just which You do not will.

And then the reasoning stops.

"But if it can at all be apprehended why Thou canst will to save the wicked; certainly that can by no means be comprehended why among those alike wicked Thou savest these rather than those by Thy supreme goodness, and condemnest those rather than these by Thy supreme justice."

ANSELM, PROSLOGION XI

Nulla ratione comprehendi potest. By no reasoning can it be comprehended.

Look at who is saying it. This is the man whose entire life's method was sola ratione. The man who set out to prove the incarnation with Christ removed from the evidence. The man who would not take an authority's word for anything he could work out himself. He runs his reason down as far as reason goes — and it does not fail at the existence of God, or the Trinity, or the necessity of the cross. Those he thinks he can walk you through. It fails at the discriminating decree: at the plain fact that of two men equally guilty, one is drawn and one is not, and the difference is not in the men.

Note also what he does not do at the wall, which is the mark of a first-rate mind. He does not soften the fact to make it manageable. He does not relocate the difference into the two men, where it would be comfortable and where every century since has tried to put it. He states it at full strength — hos magis quam illos, these rather than those, out of men alike wicked — and then admits he cannot get behind it, and stops.

Which is precisely what Paul does. Nine chapters into Romans he presses the same question to the same wall — "Therefore God has mercy on whom he wants to have mercy, and he hardens whom he wants to harden" — and two chapters later he is not arguing anymore: "Oh, the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable his judgments, and his paths beyond tracing out!" Romans 9 does not resolve into a system. It resolves into a doxology.

Two of the most relentless minds the church has ever produced, nine hundred years apart, running the argument as hard as it can be run, hit the same wall in the same place — and both do the same thing when they hit it. They stop arguing. They start worshipping. And what looks at first like the collapse of the project is the project succeeding: reason, honestly pushed, will always deliver you to something it cannot contain, and refusing to pretend otherwise is the last and highest work reason does.

The limit is not the failure. The limit is where you find out you were never the largest thing in the room.

The Man on the Stone Floor

Picture the cell at Bec in the years before any of the titles — a thin, careful man in a wool habit, stone floor cold under his knees, dawn still a couple of hours off. He has been carrying a sentence for weeks and cannot set it down. Every chapter of the book he is about to write will be addressed to God in the second person. He never once gets up off his knees to make his point. He makes it from down there.

And when the sentence finally comes, he does not congratulate himself on having caught it. He says thank you: quod prius credidi te donante, iam sic intelligo te illuminante — what I first believed because You gave it, I now understand because You lit it. Two gifts, one giver, and neither of them his.

There is a cheap version of this available and it should be refused before anyone offers it to you: that whatever you happen to feel while reading proves something about your standing with God — interest proving He is at work in you, resistance proving it too, so the case can never lose and you can never check it. That is not an argument, it is a coin with heads on both faces, and you are right to throw it back. Nothing here rests on the temperature of your response. Anselm's rested on nothing of his either. He was on the floor in the dark, wanting to stop thinking about it, and it came anyway.

So this is the honest end of the road, and it is the same road you are on. Reason will take you a long way — it took Anselm all the way to the door. What it could not tell him, and cannot tell anyone, is why the door opened for him and not for the man in the next cell, sitting through the same offices, hearing the same psalms, held or not held by a decision made before either of them was born. There is no argument at the bottom of your salvation. Not because the bottom is irrational, but because the bottom is not a proposition at all. It is a Person, and He was already there.

And that Person has a name and a reason for being here, and Anselm put the reason in the title of a later book: Cur Deus homo. Why did God become a man?

The answer he gave is a piece of engineering, and it is an answer about debt — a debt only a man owed and only God could pay, therefore a God who is a man, and the account clears. It is true as far as it reaches. But it treats the flesh as currency, the means by which a sum was settled, and if the flesh had only been currency He would have handed it back when the sum was paid.

He did not hand it back. He is a man still, and will be one forever. No payment requires the payer to permanently become the coin. So the question has a second half that accounting cannot reach: not only why God became a man, but why He stayed one.

Hebrews takes that up, and does something startling with it. It hands the Son a line from the prophets and lets Him speak it in front of His Father: "I will put my trust in him. And again, Here am I, and the children God has given me."

Look at who is talking and what He is holding. The Son does not arrive alone, and He does not arrive with an argument. He arrives with people, and He describes them in a phrase worth slowing down for: the children God has given me. Not children who found Him, not children who reasoned their way in. Given.

Which means the thing Anselm could not get behind has a second face, and hardly anyone turns it over. We say election and we picture God selecting. Hebrews says it from the receiving end: there was something the Father wanted His Son to have, and it was people. You were, in the counsel of God, a gift given to Christ. That is why the flesh is permanent. Nobody enters a family for the length of a transaction; you enter it because you intend to stay in it.

Which is why the man on the stone floor was right to stop where he stopped. He could not reason his way beneath what was holding him up, and neither can you, and neither of you was ever asked to. What was under him in the dark was a Brother who came down and took the nature you are wearing and never gave it back, who stands before His Father holding the ones He was given, and who has never once had to look down and count. That did not shift when Anselm's mind reached its limit. It does not shift on a morning when you feel nothing at all.

The floor has a name.

Go Deeper

Anselm inherited his anthropology from Augustine and handed it forward to the schools where Luther would be trained — one link in a chain that runs unbroken through the whole arc of church history. For the doctrine itself, laid out in order, start with unconditional election or the wider map of the doctrines of grace. For why the mind fights this particular truth so much harder than the others, see what it costs us to admit it. And the primary sources — Anselm's own, and five centuries of those who read him — are in the Reformed Source Library.
