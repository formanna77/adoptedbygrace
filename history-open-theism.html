Open Theism: The Heresy That Finally Said the Quiet Part Out Loud

In 1994, five Arminian theologians followed their own premises to the end. The result was a God who does not know the future. They called it "the openness of God." It is the oldest lie in Christendom finally running out of places to hide.

By Foreknown · January 1, 2026

In Brief: Open theism finally says the quiet part out loud — that God does not actually know or control the future, that He is improvising alongside you. It is meant to comfort, but it abandons you to a universe no one is steering. The mercy hidden inside the heresy is that it forces the real question: would you rather a God who hopes, or a God who reigns? Scripture answers with a Lord who works all things according to His will — including the grace that drew you. Your salvation never hung on a God crossing His fingers; it rested on the One who chose you before the foundation of the world.

The phone rings late and the voice on the other end says accident. Notice what you do not say. You do not say, "God, I know You didn't see this coming." You say — and it is out of you before you have decided to say it — God. You know.

An open theist would tell you those two words prove nothing, and on the narrow point he is right. Nobody in this debate thinks God is uninformed about an accident that has already happened. He is not arguing that God missed it. He is arguing that until a free creature acts, there is no fact there yet for anyone, God included, to have known.

So give him the two words. It is the word after them that convicts you.

Because the next thing out of your mouth is not information. It is a request. Please. Please let her live, please let them get there in time, please. And a request is not addressed to a witness. It is addressed to whoever has custody of what happens next — an outcome that has not occurred, in a room you are not in, through the hands of a surgeon whose choices are still ahead of him. You did not report to an observer. You begged an owner. Every reflex in that prayer assumes the future belongs to Somebody. That assumption is 1,600 years of theology surfacing in a single breath — and in 1994, five men wrote the book that finally denied it.

The book came out of InterVarsity Press in 1994: The Openness of God. Five authors — Clark Pinnock, John Sanders, Richard Rice, David Basinger, William Hasker — with four hundred years of Arminian theology quietly walking behind them. They did not call themselves innovators. They called themselves consistent. And they were right, in a sense no one in their movement wanted to admit out loud.

Their argument was devastatingly simple: if you really believe — as classical Arminianism has always claimed — that human beings have libertarian free will, that God never coerces a decision, and that the future is genuinely "open" to human choice, then the honest thing is to admit that God does not know the future exhaustively. He can't. Because there isn't a future to know yet. It is being made, moment by moment, by free choices that have not yet crystallized into fact. God may forecast brilliantly, like a chess grandmaster. But He does not know it the way He knows the past.

The Reformed world reacted with the kind of horror usually reserved for a barn fire. The Evangelical Theological Society spent two years deciding whether Pinnock and Sanders were even allowed to remain members. In the end neither man was expelled: expulsion required two-thirds, and Sanders drew 62.7 percent — he kept his membership by twenty-seven votes. Pinnock kept his easily. Huntington University fired Sanders the next year anyway. And through it all, the average evangelical in the pew had no idea what was happening — or why it had to happen, sooner or later, given the theological soil American evangelicalism had been planted in for two hundred years.

1,600 Years of Smuggling

To see why open theism was inevitable, you have to understand what Arminianism has always been doing quietly in the basement of its system. The classical Arminian wants two things simultaneously: libertarian free will (the ability to genuinely choose otherwise at any moment) and exhaustive divine foreknowledge (God knowing every future choice infallibly, billions of years in advance).

These two commitments are at war. If God's knowledge of your future choice is infallible and has existed since before time, then the future is fixed from the perspective of God's knowing. And a future that is fixed from any perspective is not open. An infallibly known future choice is, by definition, not a choice that could have gone otherwise. If it could have, God's knowledge would have been wrong — and God's knowledge cannot be wrong.

Arminians from Jacobus Arminius himself to modern Wesleyans have tried to escape this through "simple foreknowledge" or the Molinist category of "middle knowledge" — God's knowledge of what any free creature would do in any circumstance He might create. It is the most ingenious move anyone has ever made on this board, and it deserves the hardest form of the question rather than a joke.

So here is the question that has been put to it for four hundred years, and it is not rhetorical. What makes those counterfactuals true? Before God decrees anything, before the creature exists, before there is a world at all — what renders "if Peter were in circumstance C, he would freely deny Christ" true rather than false? Not Peter; he does not exist yet. Not the circumstance; it has not obtained. And not God — because the whole architecture depends on these truths not being up to Him. This is the grounding objection, and the Molinist has a serious answer to it.

The best version does not look for a ground. It denies that one is owed. William Lane Craig has argued for years that the demand for a truthmaker behind every truth is simply a bad rule of metaphysics, and he has a point that is easy to feel: nothing in the world makes it true that there are no unicorns. The absence of unicorns is not a thing sitting somewhere, doing the work. Why should Peter would deny need a thing behind it either? Some propositions are just true.

Grant it. Grant the whole of it, without haggling, and then look at what is left standing.

If that counterfactual is true with no ground whatsoever, then when God consults it He is not learning something about Peter. He is reading a brute fact with no explanatory connection to Peter at all — one that was true before Peter was conceived, that nothing in Peter caused, and that Peter, once conceived, has no power on earth to falsify. Middle knowledge was built to keep Peter the author of his own denial. Ungrounded, it makes him the debtor of a proposition. The determination did not disappear. It moved off God's desk and into a brute fact that loves nobody.

Which is the whole trade, once you can see both sides of it at once. Ground the counterfactuals and God has decreed them: Molinism is Calvinism in a false beard. Leave them ungrounded and Peter's act was settled before he drew breath by something with no mind, no purpose, and no stake in him. Both roads end with the denial fixed in advance. The only question the four hundred years have ever been about is who fixed it — and a man is not freer for having been determined by an accident than by a Father.

The foreknowledge objection has been inside the church since Pelagius. The Council of Orange condemned it. The Synod of Dort condemned it again. It never went away. Open theism's contribution was that it finally said: you can't keep both. One has to go. And Pinnock made his choice — he kept the free will and dropped the knowledge.

Why Your Pastor Is Probably Halfway There

Here is the shocking thing. The average evangelical pastor in America, when asked about God's sovereignty over suffering, will say things indistinguishable from open theism — and he does not know he is doing it. Listen to him at a funeral: "God didn't do this. He hates this." At a hospital bed: "God's plan A was for this not to happen." At a crisis: "God will use this for good even though He didn't want it."

Every one of those sentences denies that God is sovereign over the specific event. Every one assumes His plan was interrupted and He is now scrambling — with great love but reduced options — to salvage what He can. This is not even classical Arminianism, which at least maintained that God foreknew the event. This is, functionally, open theism, preached from an American pulpit every Sunday.

A god who was surprised by your cancer is a god who is improvising your treatment.

And notice that nobody actually wants him. The funeral-voice God — the "He didn't want this" God — is a God nobody prays to when the machines are beeping. In the daylight he is comforting, because he lets God off the hook for the tumor. At two in the afternoon in a waiting room he is unbearable, because he is off the hook for the cure as well. The one thing you came to ask him for is the one thing he does not control. Redemption requires authorship; you cannot rewrite a story you did not write. Which is why the request comes out of people anyway, aimed past the theology they were taught, at Someone who owns the ending.

"I am God, and there is no other; I am God, and there is none like me. I make known the end from the beginning, from ancient times, what is still to come. I say, 'My purpose will stand, and I will do all that I please.'"

ISAIAH 46:9-10

This verse is the execution order for open theism. If God declares the end from the beginning, then the future is not open from His side. If His purpose will stand, then no human choice is going to alter it. The only way to squeeze wiggle room into it is to decide God is speaking as a confident forecaster, not an omniscient sovereign. But once you decide that about Isaiah 46, you can decide it about anything — and your God has become a projection of the passages you find comforting.

Why This Heresy Is Actually a Mercy

Open theism did something the classical Arminian never quite managed: it made the cost of libertarian free will visible. For four centuries Arminians had been promising a God who was both fully sovereign and fully deferential to human choice. It was a beautiful promise. It was also incoherent. And as long as the incoherence stayed buried in academic footnotes, the average Arminian could enjoy the best of both worlds.

Open theism pulled the incoherence into daylight. It said: pick. If you want libertarian free will, you cannot have a God who knows the future. And once evangelicals saw the choice clearly, many recoiled — not because open theism was exegetically wrong (though it is), but because the God it produced was obviously inadequate to the God they had been worshiping. A God who does not know the future cannot choose you before the foundation of the world. He cannot promise that all things work together for good. He cannot guarantee that the shepherd will not lose any sheep. He cannot say "it is finished" at the cross and mean it.

And that makes open theism a strange gift. It is the Arminian position with its makeup off. For anyone with eyes to see, the choice is now starkly clear: either you worship the God of Scripture, who declares the end from the beginning, or you worship the limited-knowledge god of open theism, who is doing his best but cannot promise the ending. There is no third option.

Pinnock killed it, accidentally, by trying to save it.

Why This Matters for You

If you have ever said — at a hospital bed, at a funeral, at a moment of private grief — "God did not want this to happen," then you have quietly borrowed from open theism without knowing it. I am not trying to shame you. I am telling you that the sentence you borrowed belongs to a god who cannot save you, and that the God who can save you is saying something else about your life right now.

He is saying: I knew this was coming. I decreed the circumstances. I have counted every tear. Not one of them surprised me. Not one was plan B. I am the one who has been writing this story since before the stars were strung, and the ending is already fixed — and you are in it, and you are safe. That is the God of Augustine. That is the God of Spurgeon. That is the God who has been the refuge of every suffering saint who ever opened a Bible and found a sovereignty so total that even cancer has to ask permission before it enters the room.

Open theism is a small god dressed up as a loving one. He cannot hold you. He does not know whether you will make it. A God whose faithfulness depends on your response is not a God you can rest in; He is a test you are always trying not to fail. The God of Scripture — the God of unconditional election, of definite atonement, of irresistible grace — has a knowledge that is not a forecast at all.

It is a decree.

The One Man Who Saw It Coming

Set the whole argument down for a moment, because there is one night in the Gospels where somebody did have exhaustive foreknowledge of a catastrophe, and we know precisely what he did with it.

He walked toward it. John reports the arrest in a clause most readers step over on their way to the swords: "Jesus, knowing all that was going to happen to him, went out and asked them, 'Who is it you want?'"

He went out. Knowing all of it — the kiss, the courtyard, the fire, the friend who would swear before dawn that he had never met Him, the nails, the six hours, the silence where His Father's voice had always been — He crossed the garden toward the men who had come to take Him and opened the conversation Himself. Nobody made Him. Nobody found Him. He asked the soldiers who they were looking for.

That is what foreknowledge looks like when it belongs to love rather than to a database. It does not hedge, and it does not improvise, and it does not stay behind the olive trees hoping the torches turn down a different path. It goes out to meet the worst thing on the calendar and speaks first.

And it was not a late decision, taken when the situation deteriorated. Peter was there. Peter ran. Peter lied about Him three times before the night was out — and Peter is the one who wrote afterward that the Lamb "was chosen before the creation of the world, but was revealed in these last times for your sake." Before the creation of the world. The rescue was dated before the disaster was. Whatever came at you down that road, it did not get there first.

You may recognize none of this in yourself. The instinct this page opened with may be nothing you have ever felt; plenty of people in the worst hour of their lives pray nothing at all, or pray and hear the words fall flat on the floor of the room. That was never the evidence, and a God you had to feel your way toward would be one more thing to fail at. The evidence is a man crossing a garden in the dark toward a mob He could have named one by one.

He knew every step of what was coming.

He went out to meet it.
