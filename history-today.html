Where Sovereign Grace Stands Now

The global picture: a digital reformation, a wildfire on every continent, and a call to carry the torch.

By Foreknown · January 1, 2026

In Brief

You have been told the doctrines of grace are dying — the last embers of a cold European theology. You have been told a lie. The centre of gravity has already moved. The church is growing fastest in Africa, Asia and Latin America, and wherever the text is read straight through with no Western commentary on hand to soften it, Romans 9 keeps arriving at the conclusion it reached in Hippo and Geneva. And the wind carrying these truths is the same wind that carried them through Augustine's Carthage, Calvin's Geneva, and Edwards' New England — the Spirit of the God who said "My sheep listen to my voice" and meant it.

The Room and the World

Picture a church where sovereign grace is treated like a relic — a theological antique, something serious people held before the invention of the altar call. Graying heads. Empty chairs toward the back. It is an easy room to picture, because most of us have sat in some version of it, and the room hands you its conclusion without being asked: this is a small, shrinking tradition, and the world has moved on.

Which is worth pausing over, because of what has just been done. A claim about the truth of a thing has been settled by counting the people in one building.

That is the oldest mistake there is — confusing the size of your horizon with the size of the thing. So step outside the building. The view from above is no tradition guttering out. It is a fire crossing continents faster than anyone can map it, and the people most certain it is dying are standing in the one cold corner of a house already burning.

While you were measuring, the ninth chapter of Romans was being preached in Nairobi and Seoul and São Paulo by men who did not get it from the West. They got it the way anyone gets it — reading straight ahead until the ground of their own autonomy ran out beneath them, the way a man discovers a cliff he did not know was there. Across Kenya, Nigeria, Uganda, South Africa, it is the same: the continent that once received missionaries is now teaching the church what its own book says. The same thing is breaking out in Seoul and Manila and São Paulo, in house churches in China that no missionary could reach and no government can close. Different languages, different centuries of culture, no committee coordinating any of it — and the same conclusion, everywhere the text is read to the end. Geneva did not invent that conclusion any more than it invented gravity. It only wrote it down. And gravity is found in São Paulo too.

The Experiment No One Designed

For two thousand years this truth traveled at the speed of the printed page and the missionary's feet. Access to it was wildly unequal — and so you could explain who believed it by who could reach it: who owned the books, who attended the schools, who lived within walking distance of a faithful pulpit. Belief tracked access, access tracked privilege, and the whole thing looked, from the outside, like a sociology.

Then, in a single generation, the internet drove the cost of access to nearly zero. A teenager in rural India and a tenured professor in Tübingen now open the same Romans 9 on the same glowing screen. A believer in Chengdu hears the same teaching on grace as a believer in Chicago — the same hour, the same words, for free. The one variable that used to explain everything, who could get to the truth, has been flattened across the whole earth.

And the seeing did not equalize.

Sit with that, because no one designed it and it proves more than a thousand sermons could. A natural experiment has been running on a planetary scale: hold access genuinely constant — the same species reading the same words — and an unequal seeing remains. If understanding sovereign grace were a function of intelligence, the brilliant would believe; a great many of the brilliant do not, and a teenager in Kampala does. If it were a function of exposure, the most-exposed would see the most; the seminaries that have read the longest have often seen the least. If it were a function of culture, it would rise and fall with culture — yet it is guttering in the culture that exported it and blazing in the cultures that received it. Every natural cause you could name has now been controlled for. And a difference still stands.

There is a word for a difference that survives after every natural explanation has been subtracted away. Scripture supplied it long before the experiment could be run. The word is gift — and the absence of the gift is the only thing left that explains the dark. "No one can come to me," Jesus said, "unless the Father who sent me draws them." The wired, leveled, instant world has done for us what the church could never do for itself: it has run the control, and shown that sight was never about access at all.

Why This Hour

Why is the gift being poured out now, on these continents, through this medium? We are not told the whole of it; the wind is not required to file a flight plan. But the shape of the moment is suggestive.

For decades the West tried to grow the church by subtraction — a gospel with its hard edges filed off, built to ask nothing it had not first made comfortable. Put the harder question to yourself: when did you last say anything about God's sovereignty to someone who did not already agree? If the answer is slow to surface, the silence is worth looking at before it gets filed under prudence. The teenager in Lagos was never taught that caution. She opened the book, and the text was enough.

Meanwhile the people receiving this truth most hungrily are, very often, the people with the least to protect — believers under pressure, under poverty, under regimes that make following Christ costly. A faith resting on human achievement cannot survive that weather. A faith resting entirely on the achievement of God can stand in any weather at all, because nothing in it was ever load-bearing except the One who holds him — God on His throne, His purposes unthwarted, His sheep uncountable and unloseable.

The Harvest and the Plowing

Someone always raises the objection here: if God ordains who is saved, why labor at all — why preach, why pray, why carry the truth across an ocean? It is the same logic as asking why a farmer plants if God controls the weather. He plants because God ordained the harvest and the plowing — the end and every means to it. The doctrines of grace have never produced folded hands. They produce the boldest evangelists on earth, precisely because the outcome does not rest on the persuader's skill. You cannot fail to save someone you were never able to save in the first place. You can only be faithful, and watch.

So the church carries the torch — not to rescue a kingdom in danger, but because carrying it is how the King gathers the ones already His. Machen stood when the academy commanded him to sit. Lloyd-Jones traded a brilliant medical career for a pulpit in a country emptying of belief. Neither was trying to win. Each was being faithful to a victory already secured — and the distance between those two postures is the distance between exhaustion and rest.

How It Actually Happened

Movements have machinery, and this one's is unusually easy to trace — which matters, because the story people tell about the recovery of these truths is almost always a story about personalities, and the personalities came last.

It started with reprints. In 1957 a small British publishing house, the Banner of Truth Trust, began putting the Puritans back into print — books that had been effectively unobtainable for two hundred years, sold cheaply, in paperback, to a readership nobody believed existed. It turned out to exist. In 1971 an American pastor named R. C. Sproul opened a study center in Pennsylvania on the assumption that ordinary people could be taught serious theology and would want to be. That assumption was, at the time, eccentric. In 1986 a Minneapolis pastor published a book built on one sentence — God is most glorified in us when we are most satisfied in Him — and the ministry that grew up around it would eventually give away decades of sermons online, free, to anyone who came looking.

Then the cost of distribution went to zero, and the thing that had been true for four hundred years became discoverable by anyone with a search box. A young man in Lagos or Manila or a small town in Alabama could, in an afternoon, read Owen on the death of Christ, hear a sermon on Romans 9 preached by a man he would never meet, and download the whole Reformed tradition for the price of nothing. That had never been possible before in the history of the church. It is possible now, and this page exists because of it.

By 2006 the phenomenon had a name in the religious press. By 2009 a secular newsmagazine listed the return of this theology among the ten ideas then changing the world, and ranked it third — which tells you nothing about whether it is true, and a great deal about how unexpected its return was to people whose business is watching what people believe.

What no one predicted was the map. The center of gravity did not stay in the American South or the English Midlands. Reformed confessions are being subscribed in Korea, in Brazil, in Nigeria and Kenya and Uganda, in the Chinese house churches — often by pastors who found the doctrine the same way the Reformers did, by reading the text until it would not let them go, and only afterwards discovered they had joined something with a name and a history.

The Objection Worth Answering

Anyone who has watched this recovery honestly has also watched something else: a run of public collapses. Prominent men who preached sovereign grace with power turned out to be bullies, or frauds, or worse. Ministries built on a name came apart when the name did. The wreckage is real, some of it is recent, and people were hurt in it who had done nothing but trust the man at the front.

It would be dishonest to move past that quickly, and cowardly to answer it with a slogan. So answer it properly.

Ask first what the failures actually falsify. If the claim on offer were this doctrine produces superior men, every one of those collapses would count against it, and the case would be in serious trouble. But that is not the claim. The claim is that human beings are corrupt all the way down, that no amount of correct theology cures it, that sanctification is God's slow work and not a status conferred by getting the five points right, and that the last citadel of self-righteousness is very often orthodoxy itself. A movement of men who believed all that and then produced flawless leaders would be the anomaly. What actually happened is what the doctrine says happens.

That is an explanation, not an excuse, and the distinction matters. The doctrine that accounts for the failure also condemns it — more sharply, in fact, than a theology of human capability can, because it removes every ground on which a fallen leader might have claimed to be a special case. A man who taught that salvation is God's work from first to last has no standing at all to plead that he was, in his own case, the exception.

There is a final turn here, and it is the whole point. If your confidence was resting on the men — on a preacher, a conference, a voice in your headphones — then the last decade has been a demolition, and you should let it finish. These truths were never underwritten by the people who taught them to you. They were true when Geneva disgraced itself, true while the man who wrote them best lay in a ninth-century cell, true in centuries when nobody credible held them at all. Grace does not depend on its advocates. It never has. That is, in fact, the very thing grace means — and the collapses are only the doctrine proving itself on the bodies of the men who preached it.

"To him who is able to keep you from stumbling and to present you before his glorious presence without fault and with great joy — to the only God our Savior be glory, majesty, power and authority, through Jesus Christ our Lord, before all ages, now and forevermore! Amen."

And You

Here is the question that should stop you mid-scroll. The teenager in Kampala, the seminary student in São Paulo, the house-church planter in Chengdu — every one of them came to see this by reading the same Bible you can hold in your hand right now. Did they decide to see it? Then explain the millions who read the identical passages and see nothing. Were they given eyes? Then what you are watching spread across the earth is not a movement. It is a harvest — and a harvest implies a Sower who was at work in the field long before the field knew it was a field.

And the same question turns on you, though not the way you might brace for. It does not turn on how chosen you feel, and it does not turn on the fact that this page happens to be open. It turns on something Jesus said out loud, in the middle of an argument, about a map nobody in the room had ever seen: "I have other sheep that are not of this sheep pen. I must bring them also. They too will listen to my voice, and there shall be one flock and one shepherd" (John 10:16).

Read the news off that sentence. I must. Not I hope to. Not I have made it possible for them to come, should they care to. I must — because they are already Mine, every one of them chosen before they were broken, and I have never yet come home short. The house churches in Chengdu are that sentence. The seminaries in São Paulo are that sentence. So were the New England towns that woke in the 1740s. What is moving across the earth right now is not a theology winning converts. It is Jesus Christ collecting people He bought and can describe from memory.

So when the wind reaches a room, or a screen, or a person nobody was praying for, that is not weather. There is a Shepherd behind it who has counted, and knows the number, and is still walking. The wind does not blow at random. And it has never once missed.
