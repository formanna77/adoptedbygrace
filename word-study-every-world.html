The Word "World"

A close reading of kosmos across the New Testament — and the five senses the universal-atonement reading has to flatten into one.

By Foreknown · January 1, 2026

In Brief. When John writes "God so loved the world," the universal-atonement reading hears only one possible sense: "every individual human being who has ever lived." But the Greek word kosmos carries at least five distinct senses in the New Testament, and the honest work is choosing the right one from context, verse by verse. Kosmos can mean the created universe, the inhabited earth, the human race in general, the moral system organized against God, or the sweep of the nations beyond Israel. Ignore those distinctions and you break the text — John contradicts himself within two chapters. Honor them and the classic proof-texts stop proving what they are made to prove. "World" in John 3:16 is not a headcount of every individual ever born; it is the whole rebellious human order — God's love reaching past Israel into the nations — and the "whoever believes" scopes the saving of it. The God who loved that world saves particular persons from every corner of it.

The Word That Does the Heavy Lifting

If pas is the first pillar of the Arminian proof-text method (see the companion study: The Word "All"), the second pillar is kosmos. The word appears 186 times in the NT, and again and again it carries a sense a twenty-first-century reader is almost guaranteed to miss. When the missing meaning is overlooked, verses like John 3:16 and 1 John 2:2 get read as universalist proof-texts. When the meaning is recovered, those verses say exactly what Reformed theology has always said they say — and the universalist reading becomes untenable.

This page is the counterpart to the pas study. Together they close the last two lexical escape routes in the Arminian system. A reader who finishes both can no longer in good conscience cite John 3:16 or 1 Timothy 2:4 as proof of universal atonement or universal salvific will. Not because we have overthrown the text. Because the text itself refuses to play the role the system needs it to play.

The Five Meanings of Kosmos

The standard Greek lexicons (BDAG, Louw-Nida, Thayer) map several distinct domains for kosmos — the created order, the inhabited earth, humanity, the moral system in rebellion against God. To those the New Testament itself adds a fifth by the way it uses the word, especially in Paul. Here are the five as they function in the text:

  1. The created universe / cosmos — the entire material order that came into being at creation.
  2. The inhabited earth / the planet — the geographic, populated realm.
  3. The human race in general — humanity as a collective, sometimes with emphasis on its fallenness.
  4. The moral realm of unbelief / the system organized against God — the ethical sphere of darkness, sin, and rebellion.
  5. The Gentile nations / all kinds of people / people from every category — the scope beyond Israel, the "world" as the breadth of the nations.

No able defender of universal atonement reads kosmos as "every individual" in every verse — "do not love the world" would then forbid loving your neighbor. The real claim is narrower, and worth stating at its strongest: that in the decisive texts — John 3:16, 1 John 2:2 — kosmos must mean "every human being without exception." It is a serious claim, and it deserves a serious test. So give it one. Force that single sense consistently through John's own writings, and watch the text break within a few chapters.

One Short Letter, Three Different Worlds

Before we work through the examples, start with the tightest possible case. John leans on this word harder than any other New Testament writer — seventy-eight times in his Gospel, twenty-three in his first letter — and that letter runs barely five chapters. Watch it carry three different freight-loads inside one short span.

1 John 2:2 — "He is the atoning sacrifice for our sins, and not only for ours but also for the sins of the whole world." Read as every individual, this is outright universalism: every person's sin atoned, no one left to perish.

1 John 2:15, thirteen verses later in the same chapter — "Do not love the world or anything in the world." Read as every individual, John is forbidding love for human beings — the very thing Christ commanded. It cannot mean that.

1 John 5:19 — "the whole world is under the control of the evil one." Read as every individual, every believer is under Satan — yet the sentence opens, "We know that we are children of God." The contradiction would sit inside one verse.

Three uses, one letter, three different senses: a universal-sounding one at 2:2, a moral realm at 2:15, a dominion of darkness at 5:19. The same fracture runs through the Gospel, where in John 17:9 Jesus says plainly, "I am not praying for the world" — declining to pray for the very ones a universal reading says the Father loves and saves. So before a single lexicon is opened, John himself has ruled out the flat rule that "world" always means every individual. The only honest question left is which sense fits which verse. Context decides — and that is exactly the work the rest of this study does.

Thirty-Plus Examples — Letting the Text Teach Us

MEANING 1 — The Created Universe

"He was in the world, and though the world was made through him, the world did not recognize him."

JOHN 1:10

First "world" = created order. Second "world" = humanity-in-the-world. Third "world" = the people who would not know Him. Three shades of one word in a single verse. The translations cannot convey the shift without footnotes.

"In the past God spoke to our ancestors through the prophets at many times and in various ways, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son, whom he appointed heir of all things, and through whom also he made the universe [tous aiōnas]."

HEBREWS 1:1-2

Here Hebrews uses a different word (aiōnas), but compare John 1:10 — the cosmos as created order.

"The God who made the world [kosmon] and everything in it is the Lord of heaven and earth…"

ACTS 17:24

Paul in Athens: kosmos = cosmos. Not "every individual human." The universe God created and sustains.

"…chosen before the creation of the world [kosmou], but was revealed in these last times for your sake."

1 PETER 1:20

Christ was foreknown before the creation of the world — before the cosmos existed. Obviously not "before every individual existed."

"For since the creation of the world God's invisible qualities — his eternal power and divine nature — have been clearly seen…"

ROMANS 1:20

Creation order. The universe-as-designed-thing.

MEANING 2 — The Inhabited Earth

"Again, the devil took him to a very high mountain and showed him all the kingdoms of the world and their splendor."

MATTHEW 4:8

The inhabited, geopolitical world. Satan did not present Jesus with an enumeration of every individual human. He showed Him political entities — kingdoms — spread across the earth.

"And this gospel of the kingdom will be preached in the whole world as a testimony to all nations…"

MATTHEW 24:14

The Greek behind "whole world" here is oikoumenē — the inhabited earth — set in deliberate parallel with "all nations" (pasin tois ethnesin). It is a different word from kosmos, and that difference is itself the point: when the New Testament wants "the populated world," it reaches for a word that means exactly that, and the referent is geographic and ethnic scope — never a tally of every individual soul.

"In those days Caesar Augustus issued a decree that a census should be taken of the entire Roman world."

LUKE 2:1

Greek: pasan tēn oikoumenēn — the inhabited Roman world, not the planet.

"Truly I tell you, wherever this gospel is preached throughout the world, what she has done will also be told, in memory of her."

MATTHEW 26:13

Geographic reach, not individual enumeration.

MEANING 3 — Humanity in General

"Now we know that whatever the law says, it says to those who are under the law, so that every mouth may be silenced and the whole world held accountable to God."

ROMANS 3:19

Humanity as a category — Jew and Gentile alike — is held accountable. "World" here means human beings collectively as responsible moral agents.

"Therefore, just as sin entered the world through one man, and death through sin, and in this way death came to all people, because all sinned…"

ROMANS 5:12

"World" here is the realm of humanity into which sin was introduced. Compare federal headship.

"By these waters also the world of that time was deluged and destroyed."

2 PETER 3:6

Ho tote kosmos — "the world of that time." The world that drowned was people, not planets: kosmos as the human realm standing under judgment.

MEANING 4 — The Moral Realm Opposed to God

This is the meaning Arminians most consistently fail to see — and yet it is everywhere in John and the Pauline epistles. Here "world" does not mean every individual human; it means the spiritual-ethical system of rebellion, darkness, and unbelief organized against God.

"Do not love the world or anything in the world. If anyone loves the world, love for the Father is not in them."

1 JOHN 2:15

Obviously "world" here is not every human being — we are commanded elsewhere to love human beings. "World" here is the moral-ethical realm of sin and rebellion.

"They are from the world and therefore speak from the viewpoint of the world, and the world listens to them."

1 JOHN 4:5

Moral realm. Ideological alignment with rebellion.

"This is the victory that has overcome the world, even our faith."

1 JOHN 5:4

Not "we have defeated every individual." The moral system of unbelief.

"My kingdom is not of this world. If it were, my servants would fight to prevent my arrest by the Jewish leaders. But now my kingdom is from another place."

JOHN 18:36

Political-ethical realm. Jesus does not mean His kingdom is located on a different planet.

"I have told you these things, so that in me you may have peace. In this world you will have trouble. But take heart! I have overcome the world."

JOHN 16:33

The realm of hostility, rebellion, and suffering. Not every individual human.

"For everything in the world — the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life — comes not from the Father but from the world."

1 JOHN 2:16

Three specific moral pathologies. The "world" is their source. A moral system, not a population count.

"Don't you know that friendship with the world means enmity against God?"

JAMES 4:4

Ethical alignment. Friendship with sinful rebellion. James is not forbidding friendship with human beings.

"What we have received is not the spirit of the world, but the Spirit who is from God…"

1 CORINTHIANS 2:12

The animating spirit of the rebellious system.

MEANING 5 — The Gentile Nations / All Kinds of People

This sense stands behind the great salvation texts, John 3:16 and 1 John 2:2. When a first-century Jewish-Christian writer said "the world," his immediate sense was often "the nations — not just us." The gospel coming into the world meant breaking out of Israel's ethnic boundary. It is not the only defensible reading of John 3:16, as we will see — but it is a decisive one, and it rests not on a lexicon entry but on the way Paul himself pairs the words.

"So the Pharisees said to one another, 'See, this is getting us nowhere. Look how the whole world has gone after him!'"

JOHN 12:19

The Pharisees said this on the heels of Greeks (Gentiles) coming to seek Jesus in 12:20-22. "World" here is the nations — the Gentile sphere breaking in on what the Pharisees thought was Israel's exclusive territory. Verse 19 and verse 20 belong together.

"But if their transgression means riches for the world, and their loss means riches for the Gentiles, how much greater riches will their full inclusion bring!"

ROMANS 11:12

Paul lays "world" and "Gentiles" side by side as parallel terms — "their" being Israel's. This is the anchor of the fifth sense: not a separate lexicon gloss, but Paul's own equation, kosmos standing exactly where ta ethnē (the nations) stands. The next verse says it again.

"For if their rejection brought reconciliation to the world, what will their acceptance be but life from the dead?"

ROMANS 11:15

"World" here = Gentiles. Same author, same chapter, same parallel.

"Look, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!"

JOHN 1:29

John the Baptist, announcing the Messiah. This is the one verse that could sit as easily under "humanity in general" as here — and that very ambiguity makes the point. Push it toward "every individual" and universalism arrives at once, because the verb is real removal, present and effective: the Lamb takes away sin; He does not merely offer to. A sin actually lifted is a sinner actually saved — so if this "world" were every person, not one could perish. The able reply is to soften the verb to provision: He makes sin removable for all. But that is not how the text reads, and not how the lamb it echoes worked — the Passover lamb did not make death passable-over in general; it shielded the house whose doorframe wore its blood. To a Jewish audience steeped in that imagery, the scandal in the word "world" is not a headcount at all. It is that the Lamb is for the nations, and not for Israel alone.

"For God so loved the world that he gave his one and only Son, that whoever believes in him shall not perish but have eternal life."

JOHN 3:16

Here the ablest opponent deserves the floor, because the strongest reading of this verse is not the weak one usually knocked down. The weak version says "world" merely means "the big wide world, every last individual," and God's love is measured by its sheer reach. The strong version — argued by careful exegetes who are themselves Reformed, D. A. Carson and Leon Morris among them — says something more unsettling: in John, kosmos is the created human order in its rebellion, the God-hating world. On that reading the wonder of the verse is not that the world is so big it takes a great love to cover it, but that the world is so bad — so set against Him — that God loved it anyway and gave His Son. That is the objection at full strength, and it is a magnificent truth. Grant it entirely. Then notice what it still does not yield. The sentence does not stop at "loved"; it moves to a purpose — that whoever believes in him shall not perish but have eternal life. The love reaches the whole rebellious order; the saving of it runs through faith — and John will say it flatly in chapter 6: no one comes to the Son unless the Father draws him. Whether you read "world" as the nations breaking past Israel's fence or as the God-hating order loved in spite of itself, the verse never says every individual is savingly loved and finally saved. It says the love of God broke into a world that deserved none of it, and secured — through God-given faith — the eternal life of everyone who believes. See John 3:16 demolished.

"He is the atoning sacrifice for our sins, and not only for ours but also for the sins of the whole world."

1 JOHN 2:2

John writes to Jewish believers. "Ours" = Jewish Christians' sins. "The whole world" = everyone else — the Gentile nations. John is not teaching universalism; he is teaching the breadth of the atonement beyond Israel. See 1 John 2:2 demolished.

"I am the bread of life… This bread is my flesh, which I will give for the life of the world."

JOHN 6:48, 51

The same chapter fixes the limit at verse 44: "no one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws them." The "world" whose life this bread gives is bounded by the Father's drawing — categorical breadth, not a universal headcount.

"And we have seen and testify that the Father has sent his Son to be the Savior of the world."

1 JOHN 4:14

Savior of the world — Savior of people drawn from every nation. The echo of Revelation 5:9, where with his blood the Lamb "purchased for God persons from every tribe and language and people and nation," is not coincidence; it is the same thought in another key.

What This Destroys

Now we can return to the Arminian proof-texts and see what happens when we read them with a lexicon instead of a slogan.

"God so loved the world" (John 3:16): Meaning 5 — or the fallen-order reading; on either, not a census of every individual. God's love breaks past Israel into the nations, or reaches down into a world in rebellion — and the "whoever believes" scopes the saving of it. Either way, the verse is about the breadth from which, and the faith through which, Christ's people are drawn.

"The Lamb who takes away the sin of the world" (John 1:29): Meaning 5. The sin of people from every nation. Particular sins of particular persons drawn from the whole world.

"The Savior of the world" (1 John 4:14): Meaning 5. The Savior whose redemptive work extends beyond Israel to the nations.

"For the sins of the whole world" (1 John 2:2): Meaning 5. Not just "our" (Jewish believers') sins, but also the sins of Gentile believers throughout the whole world.

"Do not love the world" (1 John 2:15): Meaning 4. The moral-ethical realm of rebellion, not every individual human being.

"God was reconciling the world to himself" (2 Cor 5:19): Meaning 3 or 5. Humanity or the nations — not a literal every-individual reconciliation.

"The whole world has gone after him" (John 12:19): Meaning 2, used hyperbolically. Many people from across the Mediterranean scope.

Read with lexical awareness, not a single universal-atonement proof-text survives. The verses that seemed to shut the case for Arminianism in fact never said what Arminianism needed them to say. They said something else — and that something else is entirely consistent with sovereign election, definite atonement, and the covenant of redemption.

Three Questions That Unmake the Proof-Text

If a friend tells you "John 3:16 proves universal atonement because God loves the whole world," you do not need to argue. You only need to ask three questions.

Question 1: "When John says in 1 John 2:15, 'Do not love the world,' is he telling us not to love human beings — or is 'world' here something else?" They will say "something else." Good.

Question 2: "When John says in 1 John 5:19, 'The whole world is under the control of the evil one,' does that include believers? Or is 'world' there something else too?" They will say "something else." Good.

Question 3: "So the same author in the same two books uses kosmos in multiple distinct senses. On what lexical grounds, then, do you insist that in John 3:16 it must mean 'every individual human being without exception' rather than one of the other senses John clearly uses?"

The honest answer is: there are no lexical grounds. The universal-individual reading of John 3:16 is not drawn out of the word kosmos; it is read into it, because the system requires it there. Lift that requirement off the word and it means what John's first Jewish readers would have heard: God's love reaching past Israel to the nations, and whoever believes — from any nation — receiving eternal life.

That is the Reformed reading, and it has been for centuries. It does not rest on a lexicon settling what a lexicon cannot; it rests on John's own usage — one word bent to many senses — and on the "whoever believes" that fixes who the promise is for. On that, the text is unbending.

The Love That Was Never Hiding in a Word

If you have followed the argument this far, a quieter fear may have risen underneath it. If "world" does not mean what you always assumed — if the reach of God's love is not settled by a headcount — then the question beneath every word study surfaces on its own: does God love me?

Hear this slowly, because everything turns on it. That question was never resting on the lexical range of kosmos. It never could. A word does not love you. If your hope that God loves you was propped on reading John 3:16 as a census of every individual, then it was propped on a grammatical guess that any scholar could knock loose on a Tuesday afternoon — far too thin a thread to hang a soul on. The love of God for you, if it is real, has to rest on something a lexicon can neither give nor take.

It does. It rests on a Person held out to you now. "Whoever comes to me I will never drive away" — that is the promise, and notice it does not first ask you to prove you are elect. It asks you to come. The door is the offer of Christ, and it is genuinely open; the God who set it there does not stand behind it wishing you would stay out. Hear Him ache over the very people who would not listen: "If only you had paid attention to my commands, your peace would have been like a river." That is not a God indifferent to the lost. That is longing. He means the invitation.

So do not go hunting for your name in the ache you happen to feel. The fact that this unsettles you is not the proof, and the calm of a careless reader is not the disproof — an assurance built on the weather of your own emotions will change with the weather. Look instead at what does not move: a Savior who says come, a cross that actually saved everyone it was for, a Father who keeps what He starts. Your task is not to certify the condition of your heart. It is to come, and to keep coming, to the One held out on every page — the same One the covenant of redemption named before the world was made.

Here, then, is what the word study handed back to you, and it is more than it took. The universalist reading offered a love spread so thin it secured no one in particular — everyone loved, no one saved. What the text actually holds out is a love that fixed on real persons, drawn from every tribe and tongue and nation, and paid their debt in blood and sealed them with the Spirit — a love that does not merely wish you well but comes and gets you. That love did not begin the day you first read the verse rightly. You were loved before the word kosmos ever existed to name the world He made for His own.
