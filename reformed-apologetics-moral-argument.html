The Moral Argument — The Law You Cannot Stop Obeying

Ask the atheist whether torturing a child for fun is really, actually wrong — and watch a witness rise in him that his worldview cannot account for.

In Brief: Everyone — including the most committed atheist — lives as though some things are really wrong: not unfashionable, not evolutionarily costly, but genuinely, bindingly evil. Cruelty to a child. Genocide. Betrayal. The argument is simple and inescapable: if there is no God, there is no standard above human opinion to make anything objectively wrong; yet objective wrong plainly exists (we all know it); therefore there is a God. The atheist's escape routes — "morality evolved," "it's just my culture," "I don't need God to be good" — all collapse, because they explain why we feel moral impulses without ever making them true. The conscience that condemns the world (the law written on the heart, Romans 2:14-15) is a witness the unbeliever is borrowing from the God he denies — and a witness for the Judge to whom he is accountable, who is also the Savior who kept the law in his place.

Answer this before you read another line, and answer it the way you would answer it out loud. Is it wrong — really, actually wrong, not merely repulsive, not merely a thing your century happens to frown on — to torture a small child to death for entertainment?

You said yes. You said it before the question finished, and you did not consult anything to say it. No weighing, no survey of the culture, no tally of consequences. The answer was already in you.

Now the second question, which is the whole of this page: what makes it wrong?

Notice how much slower you are. The first answer cost you nothing and arrived with total force. The second one requires work, and whatever you produce will be weaker than the certainty it is supposed to justify. That gap — between what you know instantly and what you can account for at leisure — is the moral argument. And it does not run between you and some atheist you were planning to corner with it. It runs down the middle of you.

The argument can be stated in three lines a child could follow. First: if God does not exist, then objective moral values and duties do not exist — there is no standard higher than human opinion, evolution, or preference to make anything truly binding. Second: objective moral values and duties do exist — some things really are wrong, and we all know it, and we cannot help knowing it. Third: therefore, God exists. The argument is valid; if the two premises are true, the conclusion follows like a hammer. The whole debate is whether the premises are true — and the remarkable thing is that almost everyone, in how they actually live, already believes both.

The Law Written on the Heart

Scripture said all this long before the philosophers formalized it. Paul writes that even those who never received the written law of Moses "do by nature things required by the law... they show that the requirements of the law are written on their hearts, their consciences also bearing witness, and their thoughts sometimes accusing them and at other times even defending them" (Romans 2:14-15). The Greek for conscience is syneidēsis — a "knowing-with," an internal witness that testifies whether you have measured up to a standard you did not invent and cannot fully silence. This is why guilt is universal and untaught. No one has to be instructed that betraying a friend is shameful; the conscience delivers the verdict before the philosophy class begins. The moral law is not a cultural artifact pasted onto a blank human surface. It is engraved on the surface itself, by the One whose image that surface bears.

And note what this does to the popular objection. The atheist often presses the problem of evil as his strongest weapon: how can a good God allow such suffering? But the objection is a boomerang. To call the world's suffering a genuine outrage — not just unpleasant, but wrong, a violation of how things ought to be — is to assume a real standard of good against which the world is failing. Where did that standard come from? The atheist has smuggled in the very moral law that only a Lawgiver can supply, and then used it to argue the Lawgiver away. He cannot mount his most passionate argument against God without first borrowing God's furniture to stand on. The intensity of moral outrage at evil is not evidence against God; it is one of the loudest pieces of evidence for Him.

The Atheist's Three Doors, and Why Each Is Locked

Confronted with the binding reality of right and wrong, the unbeliever has only three doors out, and each one is locked from the inside. The first is nihilism: admit that nothing is really wrong, that "evil" is a noise we make about behaviors we dislike, that the torture of the child is, cosmically speaking, no more wrong than a spider eating a fly. This door is intellectually consistent and humanly unlivable. No one — not Nietzsche, not the coldest materialist — actually believes that the Holocaust was merely unfashionable. The man who walks through this door cannot stay in the room he enters; the first time someone wrongs him, the outrage returns, and the nihilism evaporates.

The second door is relativism: morality is real but grounded in culture or personal feeling — wrong means "wrong-for-my-society" or "wrong-by-my-lights." But this door collapses the moment you lean on it. If right and wrong are merely what a culture says, then no one could ever say a culture's morality was itself wrong — and yet we say exactly that. We do not say the society that ran death camps had a different but equally valid ethics; we say it was evil, that it should have known better, that there is a standard above all cultures by which that culture stands condemned. Relativism cannot even let you criticize slavery in another century. The reformer who says "my whole society is wrong about this" — and every moral hero in history has said it — is appealing to a law above the culture, which is the one thing relativism denies exists.

The third door is the most sophisticated: moral realism without God — the claim that objective moral facts simply exist, floating free, woven into the fabric of reality, requiring no deity. But this raises the question it cannot answer: how does a fact become an obligation? You can grant that "suffering exists" is a fact, but a fact about the world does not, by itself, place you under a duty. Duties are owed; obligations bind; "ought" implies someone to whom one is answerable. A moral law in the absence of any lawgiver is like a command with no commander, a letter no one wrote, an authority that issues from nothing and binds no one. The atheist philosopher J. L. Mackie saw this more clearly than most believers do. Objective moral values, he argued, would be metaphysically queer — entities unlike anything else in nature, carrying a built-in to-be-pursuedness that no ordinary fact possesses. And Mackie did not offer that as a concession to theism. He drew the consistent conclusion and denied that such values exist at all. That is the strongest witness available here, and it is worth stating plainly rather than quietly conscripting him: the philosopher who examined the third door most carefully walked back out of it and took the first one instead, because he could see that a universe with no God in it has nowhere to put a real obligation. He was right about the architecture. We simply think he gave up the wrong item.

Evolution Explains My Conscience, and Euthyphro Explains Away Your God

The unbeliever's best replies deserve their strongest form. He says: "Morality is fully explained by evolution — cooperation and empathy helped our ancestors survive and reproduce, so we evolved the feelings we now dignify as 'conscience.' I don't need a God to be good; plenty of atheists are kinder than plenty of Christians. And your Lawgiver faces the ancient Euthyphro dilemma: is something good because God commands it — in which case morality is arbitrary, and He could have commanded cruelty — or does God command it because it is good — in which case goodness stands above God and you don't need Him after all?" These are serious, and the honest apologist grants what is true in each. Evolution surely shaped our moral psychology; atheists genuinely are, very often, decent and loving people; and the Euthyphro has embarrassed shallow theism for two and a half thousand years. Concede all of it without flinching.

Take the evolutionary account first, because it is the one with real teeth — and the teeth are pointing the other way. Suppose we did evolve to feel that murder is wrong because tribes that frowned on murder out-survived tribes that did not. That explains why we carry the impulse. It does precisely nothing to make the impulse true. On this account "murder is wrong" has the standing of "I crave sugar": a useful inherited drive, not a report about reality.

The atheist philosopher Sharon Street pressed exactly this into a dilemma, and she aimed it at her own side. If our evaluative faculties were shaped by natural selection, then either selection was tracking independent moral truths, or it was not. It was not: selection tracks what leaves descendants, and there is no reason a fitness-maximizing faculty should also happen to be a truth-detector about obligation. But if it was not tracking them, then our moral beliefs are the output of a process indifferent to whether they are correct — and no one should trust such a faculty about anything.

Street's conclusion was that moral realism is the casualty. Notice which realism. The dilemma bites a universe where the faculty was assembled by a blind process for a purpose unrelated to truth. It does not bite a universe where the faculty was made by the One whose character the truths describe, for the express purpose of recognizing them. Theism is not embarrassed by the debunking argument. Theism is the only position on the board that supplies the missing premise the debunking argument demands — a reason why the instrument should be expected to read the thing it is pointed at.

Which leaves "I can be good without God" — a slogan that answers a question nobody asked. Whether an unbeliever can know and do good: of course he can, and Romans 2 predicted it, since the law is written on his heart too. Whether anything makes good real: the slogan never goes near it. The argument was never that unbelievers behave badly. It is that no one, believer or not, can account for the standard by which anyone behaves well — and the ones who deny the Lawgiver are living on borrowed capital and calling it earned.

The Euthyphro is the last door, and it is the one where honest theists have most often given a shallow answer. The standard reply is that goodness is neither above God nor decreed by God but identical to His nature: His holiness, His love, His justice, from which His commands necessarily flow, so that He could not command cruelty because cruelty contradicts what He is.

That reply is true, and by itself it is not enough, because the sharp objector has one more move and he will make it. Fine — is God's nature good because it is His, or does His nature meet some standard of goodness? If the first, arbitrariness has simply been relocated one floor down. If the second, the standard is still above Him. The dilemma looks like it has been rebuilt inside the answer.

It has not, and the reason is worth slowing down for. The objection assumes that goodness is a property — something that attaches to a thing, so that we can always ask whether it attaches rightly. Grant that assumption and the regress never ends, for anything. But the Christian claim is not that God possesses goodness in unusually large quantities. It is that goodness is the name of what He is. Asking whether His nature meets the standard of goodness is asking whether a thing is identical to itself; you can form the sentence, but there is no question inside it.

And press the objector's demand one turn further, because it is selectively applied. Every account of anything terminates somewhere in a reality not explained by something behind it — for the naturalist, in the brute existence of physical law, which is asserted and not derived. The demand but what grounds God's goodness? insists on a further floor beneath the ultimate, which is not a requirement anyone meets or could meet. Explanation stops somewhere or nothing is ever explained. The only live question is what it stops at: an impersonal brute fact that owes you nothing and knows nothing about you, or a Person. The dilemma offered two horns; the Christian was always sitting between them, on the seat that had been there the whole time.

The Demolition, and the Catch Beneath It

So the moral argument does its demolition work: it shows the unbeliever that the moral law he cannot stop obeying is a witness against the autonomy he claims — that he did not invent the standard, cannot escape it, and cannot ground it on his own terms. But this site never leaves a reader in the rubble, and the moral argument has a catch beneath it deeper than any debate. For if the argument is sound, then the moral law is real, and you have broken it. Not someone else — you. The same conscience that you appealed to when you condemned the world's cruelty has, in its quieter hours, condemned you: for the cruelty you have done, the love you have withheld, the standard you affirm with your mouth and violate with your life. The moral argument is not finally a clever proof to win a debate. It is a mirror, and the face in it is your own, and the verdict it returns is guilty.

That is exactly where the gospel meets you, and only there. The Lawgiver against whom you have sinned did not stay on the bench. He came down. The eternal Son took on flesh and kept the whole law you have broken — every command, from the heart, without a single failure — and then, at the cross, He took the penalty the law pronounced on lawbreakers, dying in the place of the very people who had spent their lives suppressing the truth about Him. The conscience that accuses you was never meant to be your final judge; it was meant to drive you to the One who satisfied the law and bore its curse. In Christ, the guilty are declared righteous — not because the law was lowered, but because it was kept and its sentence served, by Another, for you. The moral argument ends not in "therefore try harder to be good," which would only deepen the despair, but in "therefore flee to the One who was good in your place."

Which is why the argument you came here to win turns out to be an argument you lose. You were right about the child. You were right that the wrongness was real and not a preference and not a fashion. You were right, and being right is what convicts you, because the standard you were so sure of is the standard you have spent your life failing, and a witness you cannot impeach has been filing the report the whole time.

You could never silence that witness. You were never going to satisfy it. The moral argument ends where every honest conscience ends, with an accusation no one can answer.

And then Jesus Christ answers it.
