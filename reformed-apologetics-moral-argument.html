The Moral Argument — The Law You Cannot Stop Obeying

You know that cruelty to a child is wrong the way you know your own name — and nothing else you believe about the universe entitles you to that certainty.

In Brief: Everyone lives as though some things are really wrong: not unfashionable, not evolutionarily costly, but genuinely, bindingly evil. Cruelty to a child. Genocide. Betrayal. The argument runs in three lines: if there is no God, there is no standard above human opinion to make anything objectively wrong; objective wrong exists; therefore God exists. The escape routes — morality evolved, it is my culture's construct, I can be good without God — all explain why we feel moral impulses without ever making one of them true. A few serious philosophers accept that consequence and deny that anything is really wrong. Almost none can inhabit it for an afternoon. The conscience that condemns the world (the law written on the heart, Romans 2:14-15) is borrowed from the God it is used to argue against, and it testifies for the Judge to whom you are accountable, who is also the Savior who kept the law in your place.

Answer this before you read another line, and answer it the way you would answer it out loud. Is it wrong — really, actually wrong, not merely repulsive, not merely a thing your century happens to frown on — to torture a small child to death for entertainment?

You said yes. You said it before the question finished, and you did not consult anything to say it. And if you are careful, you will want to add something: that your yes was an attitude and not a report, a reaction you have and not a fact you found. Hold on to that. It is the most serious sentence anyone will say on this page, and we are coming back to whether you can live inside it.

Now the second question, which is the whole of this page: what makes it wrong?

Notice how much slower you are. The first answer cost you nothing and arrived with total force. The second one requires work, and whatever you produce will be weaker than the certainty it is supposed to justify. That gap — between what you know instantly and what you can account for at leisure — is the moral argument. And it does not run between you and some atheist you were planning to corner with it. It runs down the middle of you.

The argument itself takes three lines a child could follow. If God does not exist, objective moral values and duties do not exist: no standard higher than opinion, evolution, or preference makes anything truly binding. Objective moral values and duties do exist. Therefore God exists. The form is valid, so everything rides on the premises, and the remarkable thing is that almost everyone, in how they actually live, already believes both.

The Law Written on the Heart

Scripture said all this long before the philosophers formalized it. Paul writes that even those who never received the written law of Moses "do by nature things required by the law... they show that the requirements of the law are written on their hearts, their consciences also bearing witness, and their thoughts sometimes accusing them and at other times even defending them" (Romans 2:14-15). The word rendered conscience is syneidēsis, a knowing-with: a second knower inside the one knower, testifying whether you have measured up to a standard you did not set. It knows the good and it names the good. What it has no power whatever to do is produce it. Paul's argument here is narrower than it is often made to be, and worth keeping narrow: he is explaining how people who never stood at Sinai are nevertheless accountable. The moral law, on his account, is not a cultural artifact applied to a blank human surface. It is already on the surface. That is the claim. The rest of this page is about whether anything else can explain it.

Notice what this does to the popular objection. The strongest weapon against God is usually the problem of evil: how can a good God allow this much suffering? But the objection is a boomerang. To call the world's suffering a genuine outrage, not merely unpleasant but wrong, a violation of how things ought to be, is to assume a real standard the world is failing to meet. Where did that standard come from? You cannot mount your most passionate argument against God without first borrowing God's furniture to stand on. The outrage is real and it is right. It is also the exhibit.

You Have Three Doors Out, and Each Is Locked From the Inside

The first is nihilism: nothing is really wrong, "evil" is a noise we make about behavior we dislike, and the torture of the child is, cosmically speaking, no more wrong than a spider eating a fly. This door is intellectually consistent, and people genuinely walk through it. The error theorist is not a strawman and does not deserve to be told that he secretly agrees with us. The difficulty is not that the position cannot be held. It is that it cannot be inhabited. In the study it is a thesis; in the moment you are wronged it is nothing at all, because indignation is not a feeling about a rearrangement of matter, it is a claim about what someone owed you. J. L. Mackie opened Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong with the bare declaration that there are no objective values, and then spent the rest of the book building a morality anyway: obligations, rights, the point of the whole enterprise. He was not being inconsistent. He thought morality was a human device worth constructing well. But watch what a man does the moment the demolition is finished. He starts laying floor.

The second door is relativism: morality is real but grounded in culture or in your own feeling, so that wrong means wrong-for-my-society or wrong-by-my-lights. It collapses the moment you lean on it. If right and wrong are whatever a culture says, you can never say that a culture's morality was itself wrong, and you say exactly that. You do not say the society that ran the death camps had a different but equally valid ethics. You say it was evil, that it should have known better, that there was a standard above it by which it stands condemned. Relativism cannot even let you criticize slavery in another century. Every moral reformer in history has said my whole society is wrong about this, and that sentence appeals to a law above the culture, which is the one thing relativism says is not there.

The third door is the most sophisticated: moral realism without God, the claim that objective moral facts simply exist, woven into the fabric of reality, requiring no deity. Take it seriously. It is held by first-rate philosophers and it is not obviously false. But it owes an answer to a question it keeps postponing: how does a fact become an obligation? That suffering exists is a fact. A fact about the world does not, by itself, put you under a duty. Duties are owed. Obligations bind. Ought implies someone to whom one is answerable, and a moral law with no lawgiver is a command with no commander, an authority issuing from nothing and addressed to no one.

Mackie saw this more clearly than most believers do. Objective values, he argued, would be metaphysically queer: entities unlike anything else in nature, carrying a built-in to-be-pursuedness that no ordinary fact possesses, and requiring a faculty of moral perception unlike any other faculty we have. He did not offer that as a concession to theism. He drew the consistent conclusion and denied that such values exist. The philosopher who examined the third door most carefully walked back out of it and took the first one instead, because he could see that a universe with no God in it has nowhere to put a real obligation. He was right about the architecture. We simply think he gave up the wrong item.

And then he built the room again. That is the part worth sitting with, and it is not a debating point. A first-rate mind followed an argument to its end, arrived somewhere he had no reason to doubt he belonged, and could not live in the house his own reasoning had built, so he spent the remainder of the book furnishing a house he had just proved was not standing there. Most of us have a smaller version of this: a conclusion we can defend and cannot feel, or one we have argued our way out of and cannot stop obeying. There are conclusions a man can prove and cannot occupy. The inability is not a weakness in the man. It is a fact about the conclusion.

Evolution Explains My Conscience, and Euthyphro Explains Away Your God

The best replies deserve their strongest form, so here they are in your own voice. Morality is fully explained by evolution: cooperation and empathy helped our ancestors survive and reproduce, so we evolved the feelings we now dignify as conscience. I don't need a God to be good, and plenty of atheists are kinder than plenty of Christians. And your Lawgiver faces the ancient Euthyphro dilemma. Is a thing good because God commands it, in which case morality is arbitrary and He could have commanded cruelty? Or does God command it because it is good, in which case goodness stands above God and you do not need Him after all?

All three are serious, and the honest answer grants what is true in each. Evolution surely shaped our moral psychology. Unbelievers are very often decent and generous and self-sacrificing people, and a theology that denies it is refuted by every hospital ward and every good neighbor. The Euthyphro has embarrassed shallow theism for two and a half thousand years. Concede all of it without flinching.

Take evolution first, because it is the one with real teeth, and the teeth point the other way. Suppose we did evolve to feel that murder is wrong because tribes that frowned on murder out-survived tribes that did not. That explains why we carry the impulse. It does precisely nothing to make the impulse true. On this account, "murder is wrong" has the standing of "I crave sugar": an inherited drive you did not choose, running in a will you did not design, reporting on nothing outside itself. The explanation succeeds. In succeeding, it removes the thing it was called in to account for.

Sharon Street pressed this into a dilemma aimed squarely at moral realists, and the half version everyone quotes is not the dangerous one. Our evaluative faculties were shaped by selection. Either the pressures were tracking mind-independent evaluative truths, or they were not. If they were not, our judgments are the output of a process indifferent to their accuracy. That is the horn everyone repeats. The second horn does the real work: Street takes the tracking hypothesis seriously and argues it loses, as science, to the alternative — that we judge as we do because those judgments forged useful behavioral links, care for offspring, reciprocity, an aversion to being harmed, with no reference to any independent truth about value. Simpler, clearer about its mechanism, same data explained. Tracking is not an unproven hypothesis on her view. It is a worse one. Her conclusion is bounded, too: she is arguing about evaluative judgment, not arithmetic or perception, and she is an anti-realist aiming at realists, not a realist undermining herself.

Notice which realism takes the hit. The dilemma bites a universe where the faculty was assembled by a blind process for a purpose unrelated to truth. The theistic reply is that the faculty was made by the One whose character the truths describe, for the express purpose of recognizing them, and that reply is a tracking account. Which means Street's second horn is aimed at us, and has to be answered on its own ground. Her case against tracking is that a truth-tracking mechanism is an extra posit doing no work the adaptive-link account does not do more cheaply. Against a naturalist who needs the mechanism to arise on its own, that is close to decisive. Against a maker who intended the correspondence, it is not an objection at all, because the correspondence is not a further entity requiring its own explanation. It is the design specification of the faculty and the character of the ground, in one act. Naturalism already owes an account of how physics produced a knower at all. Street's dilemma is the bill arriving for the moral half of that debt.

Theism is not the only view that has noticed the problem. Godless realists have answers, and the best of them are third-factor replies: David Enoch argues that survival is itself pro tanto good, so a process selecting for survival-promoting attitudes would produce roughly correct evaluative beliefs without tracking anything; Erik Wielenberg argues that the very properties making an act wrong also, by metaphysical necessity, give rise to our belief that it is wrong. Both are serious. Neither is a dodge. But notice what each has to buy. On their own account moral facts are causally inert — they make nothing happen — so the brute facts that make cruelty wrong and the physical processes that produced our conviction about cruelty must line up exactly, with no causal traffic between them, because a further brute necessity happens to link them. Not a coincidence, on their view. Still one more unexplained alignment stacked on the first. Theism has no alignment to arrange: the maker of the faculty and the ground of the truths are the same being. Every rival must posit the fit and then explain it. Theism is the one position where the fit is not a further fact at all.

There is a prize here that apologetics keeps walking past. Street's conclusion, that if our faculties are the product of blind selection we have no business claiming knowledge of mind-independent moral truth, is premise one of the moral argument, argued by someone with no interest whatever in premise three. The most rigorous contemporary defense of "if there is no God, objective moral values do not exist" is being written by atheists, published in the best journals, and filed under victory.

Which leaves "I can be good without God", a slogan that answers a question nobody asked. Can an unbeliever know and do good? Of course, and Romans 2 predicted it, since the law is written on his heart too. Does anything make good real? The slogan never goes near it. The argument was never that unbelievers behave badly. It is that no one, believer or not, can account for the standard by which anyone behaves well, and if you deny the Lawgiver you are living on borrowed capital and calling it earned.

What Kind of Floor Is at the Bottom

The Euthyphro is the last door, and it is the one where honest theists have most often given a shallow answer. The standard reply is that goodness is neither above God nor decreed by God but identical to His nature: His holiness, His love, His justice, from which His commands necessarily flow, so that He could not command cruelty because cruelty contradicts what He is.

That reply is true, and by itself it is not enough, because the sharp objector has one more move and he will make it. Is God's nature good because it is His, or does His nature meet some standard of goodness? If the first, arbitrariness has simply moved one floor down. If the second, the standard is still above Him.

The objection assumes that goodness is a property, something that attaches to a thing, so that we can always ask whether it attaches rightly. Grant that assumption and the regress never ends, for anything at all. The Christian claim is not that God possesses goodness in unusually large quantities. It is that goodness is the name of what He is. On that claim, asking whether His nature meets the standard of goodness is asking whether a thing is identical to itself.

Here is where the argument has usually stopped, and stopping here loses it, because the identity move is not proprietary. The naturalist can run it too. Pleasure just is goodness. Flourishing just is goodness. Say either, and the follow-up question dies exactly the same death. And if we also observe, truly enough, that every account of anything terminates in something not explained by anything behind it, that the naturalist's physical constants are asserted and not derived, then we have conceded that ending at a brute ultimate is a respectable place to stop. Put those two concessions side by side and we have argued our way to a tie. A tie is a loss. Premise one does not need parity with naturalism. It needs an asymmetry.

The asymmetry is not about whether an account stops. Every account stops. It is about what kind of thing it stops at, and the candidates are not interchangeable, because obligation is not the sort of thing that can float. Look at what an obligation actually does. It is owed, to someone. It is violated, against someone. And it can be forgiven, which is the strangest and most decisive feature in the whole category, because forgiveness is not an operation a fact can perform. Now run that test down the list of candidate ultimates. Pleasure cannot be wronged. Flourishing has no standing to release you. A brute normative fact, however necessarily it exists, cannot be answered to and cannot let anything go; it sits there being true while you break it, the way the number seven sits there while you miscount. That is not what happened in you the last time you were betrayed. You did not detect a violated regularity. You were wronged, and you knew, without being taught, that someone owed you something and had not paid. That grammar is not decoration laid over morality. It is morality's actual shape, and only one kind of ultimate has it. Which is not a demand for a further floor beneath the floor. Everyone concedes there is a floor. It is a question about the character of the one we are all already standing on, and a floor that can forgive you is not the same kind of floor as one that cannot.

One objection remains, and it should have been pressed harder than the Euthyphro ever was. If goodness is the name of what God is, then God is good is true by definition, and a sentence true by definition tells you nothing. Worse: nothing He could conceivably do would count against it. That charge would land if the divine nature were stipulated. It is not. It is disclosed, and the distance between those two words is the distance between a definition and a life. Nobody learns what God's goodness consists of by unpacking a term. You learn it the way you learn what any person is like, from what He has done, on the record, in front of witnesses, over a long time and at cost. Which is why is the God of the Bible good? is not a malformed sentence but one of the most-asked questions in the history of theology, and Scripture never once treats it as a category mistake. It answers it. It answers it at a specific place, on a specific afternoon, when the standard everybody had been arguing about took a body and walked out to a hill and died there for the people breaking it. You may look at that and conclude the standard is monstrous. What you may not say is that there is nothing there to look at.

So the dilemma offered two horns and the Christian was never on either. The question was never whether explanation stops. It is what it stops at: something that cannot know you, or Someone who does.

You Were Right, and Being Right Is What Convicts You

The argument has been doing demolition work the whole time, and not on somebody else. The moral law you cannot stop obeying is a witness against the autonomy you claim: you did not invent the standard and cannot get out from under it, and you cannot ground it on your own terms. Now the second edge. If the argument is sound, the law is real, and you have broken it. Not humanity in the aggregate. You. The same conscience you appealed to when you condemned the world's cruelty has, in its quieter hours, condemned you: for the cruelty you have done, the love you withheld, the standard you affirm with your mouth and violate with your week.

Which is why the argument you came here to win is one you lose by winning. You were right about the child. You were right that the wrongness was real, and not a preference, and not a fashion. You were right, and being right is the entire indictment, because a standard binds the people who fall under it, and you have spent your life falling. The witness you called to the stand has been filing its report the whole time. You could never silence it. You were never going to satisfy it. A conscience that knows the good and cannot produce it is not a moral compass. It is a diagnosis.

That is exactly where the gospel meets you, and only there. The truth that tears the floor out is the same truth that catches you. The Lawgiver against whom you have sinned did not stay on the bench. He came down. The eternal Son took on flesh and kept the whole law you have broken, every command, from the heart, without one failure, and then, at the cross, took the penalty the law pronounces on lawbreakers, dying in the place of the very people who had spent their lives suppressing the truth about Him.

Paul names that suppression and names what it costs: "The wrath of God is being revealed from heaven against all the godlessness and wickedness of people, who suppress the truth by their wickedness, since what may be known about God is plain to them, because God has made it plain to them. For since the creation of the world God's invisible qualities — his eternal power and divine nature — have been clearly seen, being understood from what has been made, so that people are without excuse." Plain to them. Not inferred, not assembled out of evidence. Plain. Which is why the answer you gave in the first paragraph came so fast, and why it will not stay inside the box you built for it. It was an attitude, yes. It was also a leak.

The conscience that accuses you was never meant to be your final judge. It was meant to drive you to the One who satisfied the law and bore its curse. In Christ the guilty are declared righteous, not because the law was lowered, but because it was kept, and its sentence served, by Another, in your place. So the moral argument does not end in therefore try harder to be good, which only lengthens the sentence. It sends you to the One who was good in your place.

Every honest conscience arrives in the end at the same place: an accusation no one can answer.

And then Jesus Christ answers it.
