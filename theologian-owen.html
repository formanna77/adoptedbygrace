Theologian Profile

John Owen

(1616–1683) — The man whose every child died, who proved that Christ does not lose one of His own.
In Brief: John Owen (1616–1683) was Vice-Chancellor of Oxford, Cromwell's chaplain, and the author of The Death of Death in the Death of Christ — three hundred pages on one question: for whom did Christ die? His answer is that Christ did not die trying and did not die offering. He died saving — actually, particularly, finally — a people the Father had given Him before the foundation of the world. The doctrine sounds restrictive only from outside the wound. Owen, who buried all eleven of his children, wrote it from inside one.

He Buried All Eleven

John Owen and his wife Mary had eleven children. Ten died in infancy. The eleventh, a daughter, lived long enough to marry, and then consumption took her too. The man standing at those graves was Vice-Chancellor of Oxford, Cromwell's chosen chaplain, and one of the most formidable minds English Puritanism produced. None of it was any use to him at a graveside. He stood at ten small graves, and then at one that was not small. He buried Mary as well.

The book he is known for was published in 1647, when he was thirty-one and the burials had already begun. The Death of Death in the Death of Christ argues that not one of the souls for whom Christ died has ever been lost, or can be. On that account the cross is an act, not an offer: by it a particular people — chosen before the foundation of the world, given to the Son by the Father — were saved. Not made savable. Saved.

Any man can write that at thirty-one. Owen went on holding it through funeral after funeral for the next thirty-six years, and never took a word of it back.

The Boy Converted by a Man Nobody Can Name

Owen was born in 1616 at Stadhampton in Oxfordshire, entered Queen's College at twelve, and took his MA at nineteen. The one thing he could not get by application was a converted heart, and the way he finally got one is his whole theology in miniature.

He walked to Aldermanbury Chapel in London to hear a famous preacher and found a substitute in the pulpit — a country minister whose name nobody wrote down and nobody has since recovered. The man preached on Matthew 8:26, which in the Bible of Owen's century read why are ye fearful, O ye of little faith? Owen went home converted. The eternal counsel had moved an anonymous man into a London pulpit on an ordinary Sunday to fetch the soul of the man who would spend his life proving that the eternal counsel fetches whom it means to fetch.

After that the ladders came quickly. Dean of Christ Church, Oxford, in 1651. Vice-Chancellor of the University from 1652. Cromwell's chaplain, a preacher before Parliament the day after the execution of Charles I, an architect of the religious settlement of the Protectorate. He laid the Vice-Chancellorship down in October 1657; in March 1660 the deanery went as well, and the Restoration took the rest within the year. Two years after that, on 24 August 1662, the Act of Uniformity ejected some two thousand other Puritan ministers in the Great Ejection. Owen spent his last two decades pastoring a small Independent congregation in London, declining a call to New England and, later, the inducements offered him to conform, writing the books that outlasted every institution that had discarded him. By any worldly measure those twenty years were a long descent. By the measure he cared about, they were the most fruitful of his life.

He had been at the top of every ladder his world recognized. He had also stood at eleven graves. He knew what the ladders were worth.

Open Your Bible to John 6:39

Open your Bible to John 6:39. And this is the will of him who sent me, that I shall lose none of all those he has given me, but raise them up at the last day. The NIV is faithful; the Greek is simply tighter than English can be. The verb is apollumi — perish, destroy, be lost — the word Jesus uses for the sheep that wandered off in Luke 15, and the word John uses in 3:16 for what the love of God prevents: should not perish.

Then the shape of the sentence, which is where the argument lives. An English reader hears that I shall lose none as a statement of aim, the outcome the Father is hoping for. The Greek will not carry that. Touto … estin to thelēma … hina …: the hina clause stands in apposition to touto, "this," and spells out what the will consists of. By the first century hina had broadened far past purpose, and after a noun like thelēma it supplies content, not intention. The sentence does not say the Father is aiming at losing none. It says losing none is what the Father's will is.

Which is harder to answer, not easier. An aim can be held and missed; all of us intend what we fail to do. But the verse immediately before has already said what the Son came for — For I have come down from heaven not to do my will but to do the will of him who sent me — and the will He came to do is now defined as losing nobody. One soul lost is not a purpose frustrated. It is that sentence made false.

I shall lose none.

This is the verse on which Owen built. The argument of The Death of Death is exegesis, prosecuted across three hundred pages and reducible at last to this: a Christ who came to do a will He did not do is a Christ John 6:39 does not know. Either His purpose was efficacious, or it was not His purpose. John 6 has no exit door.

Pair it with the verse Owen quotes in the same breath. Acts 13:48: all who were appointed for eternal life believed. Ēsan tetagmenoi — had been appointed, complete and standing before the believing. The form also reads as a middle, and the whole opposing case lives there: as many as had disposed themselves to eternal life believed. Luke answers that two verses earlier. Describing men who position themselves, he reaches for an explicit reflexive — the Jews who do not consider yourselves worthy of eternal life. He knows how to say they did this to themselves. Of the Gentiles he does not.

Christ in the Gospel and Luke in the Acts, one conclusion. A particular people, named in eternity past, are given to Christ, are appointed to life, and believe because they were appointed — and Christ refuses to lose one of them.

Owen's Question Has Only Three Answers

Owen poses a question that has the structure of a clamp. For whom did Christ die? He says there are only three possible answers. He invites you to choose one and watch where it leads.

Choose first that Christ died for all the sins of all people. Here the ablest objector answers at once, and he should be allowed to finish. The death is sufficient for all and efficient for those who believe. Nobody claims the cross is applied to a man who never comes to it. The payment is offered; faith receives it. Press him and he goes further, because the strongest form of this case has never rested on the offer alone. Richard Baxter argued it against Owen inside Owen's own lifetime, and the hypothetical universalists have argued it since: Christ's death removed the legal bar for every man; God then appointed faith, by His own decree, as the condition on which the removal is applied. The unbeliever is not punished for a sin already paid. He is refused a benefit whose one appointed condition he never met. That is the objection at full strength, and it is not the caricature its opponents usually swing at.

Owen's reply is a single question about unbelief. But this unbelief, is it a sin or not? If not, why should they be punished for it? If it be, then Christ underwent the punishment due to it, or not. If so, then why must that hinder them more than their other sins for which he died from partaking of the fruit of his death?

Everything turns on what kind of thing the cross was, and that is the premise the objection quietly denies. If Christ's death was a quantity of suffering paid into a fund, the reply holds: a fund can sit unclaimed, and unclaimed is not unpaid. But penal substitution is not a deposit. It is one person standing in the place of another — Owen's phrase is in their stead and room — and what a substitute bears is not a measure of pain but the penalties of named persons. If the penalty due a particular man's unbelief was actually borne, God does not afterwards collect it from the man as well.

Say instead that the death lifted the legal bar for everyone and God appointed faith as the condition of applying it, and the problem does not disappear. It moves. Who supplies the condition? If God gives faith, He gives it to those He determined to give it to — and Christ has died for multitudes the Father never meant to hand Him, the Son purchasing what the Father decreed not to apply, the one will of God pulling apart at the cross. If God does not give faith, the decisive act is the man's. Either way the deciding has been lifted off the cross and set down somewhere safer. The first answer does not survive its own premise.

Choose second that Christ died for some sins of all people. Then the covering is partial, and every soul — including the ones who think themselves saved — stands at last before God with the uncovered remainder. Whatever sin you still need covered is the sin Christ did not bear. Nobody holds this one, and the reason nobody holds it is that it is the first answer with its politeness removed.

Choose third that Christ died for all the sins of some people — the people the Father gave Him in eternity past, the people Christ Himself prayed for in John 17 while explicitly refusing to pray for the world (v. 9), the people Acts 13:48 says were appointed for eternal life, the people Romans 8:30 says God glorified — stated as a done thing, in the same string of verbs as called and justified, of something not one of them has yet lived to see — the people John 10 calls Christ's sheep as a class distinct from those who refuse to believe because they are not of His sheep. Then the cross is the act it claims to be. It is finished means it is finished. The death has done what it came to do. Christ has lost none.

Two answers collapse under their own weight. The third is the gospel.

Not a Smaller Cross — a Finished One

The world hears limited atonement and recoils; it hears restrictive, it hears cold. The words the doctrine prefers — definite atonement, particular redemption — name what is actually at issue. A Christ who died for everyone in such a way that He saves no one in particular is not loving anyone particularly. He is offering a transaction in air, conditional on a will too dead in sin to supply the condition. At the exact moment the doctrine needs to do its saving work, a general atonement outsources the power to the corpse it came to raise. Owen saw this in 1647 and has been answered many times since — by Baxter within his lifetime, by abler men after him, and the argument is still being worked over in print. The answers differ considerably from one another. What every one of them has to find is somewhere for the deciding to happen that is not the cross.

The doctrine sounds cold from the outside. From the inside it is the only doctrine warm enough to bring to a grave. Because at a grave the only question left is whether the work was done. And the answer Owen gives — the answer he had to give, because he had stood at every one of those eleven graves — is that for every soul Christ purposed to save, the work was done, the price was paid, the rescue is real. Definite atonement is the doctrine of a Christ who comes home with His sheep.

Notice what this does to the gospel as it is sometimes preached. The popular invitation says: Christ has done His part — now you must do yours. The cross paid for sins, but its application waits on your decision. Owen heard that sermon in his century and called it what it is, a covert works-righteousness with a kind face. On that account the decisive act in salvation is the human will choosing the cross. Owen would not have it. The cross is not a coupon you redeem. The cross is the rescue. The same God who paid the price applies the price, by the Spirit, to the people the Son died for. The whole arc is one act, and the actor is God.

The Same Grace That Saved You Will Not Leave You Alone

The man who wrote Death of Death also wrote The Mortification of Sin, and read together they show what Owen was: a theologian of the precise interior. Be killing sin, or it will be killing you. He meant it anatomically. Owen had looked inside himself, at the regenerate self regeneration had made him, and found that sin in a Christian behaves like a living tenant. It cannot be evicted by one act of the will. It has to be put to death daily, by the only weapon that can do the killing — the Spirit applying the cross to a will the Spirit has already changed. What Owen calls for is impossible apart from regeneration and inescapable in its presence. It is what new life looks like from the inside, sweating.

Here Owen quietly answers the objection that haunts the doctrines of grace: if Christ has already saved me, why fight sin at all? His answer is structural. The Christ who saved you saved you into a new nature whose first impulse is to fight what killed it. Mortification is the symptom of the standing, never its cause. Owen will not separate justification from sanctification — the same union with Christ grounds both — and he will not collapse them either. Justification is a verdict pronounced once, fixed, complete. Sanctification is the lifelong working out of what the verdict has made you. The first is the rock. The second is the path on the rock.

Communion with God — Owen's other crown — is the same theology turned outward. Each Person of the Trinity is communed with distinctly: the Father as fountain, the Son as Mediator, the Spirit as the One who fastens the soul to both. Owen refuses to flatten the three into a vague God-feeling, because Scripture does not; and in keeping the Persons distinct he keeps the love distinct, so that it stays specific. Specific love is the only love that can survive a graveside. Generic love evaporates in the heat. Owen knew this because he had stood there.

The Eleven Graves and the Doctrine That Could Stand at Them

Now return to the eleven graves, because there is a question every careful reader of Owen eventually asks. How did the man whose every child died write the book that says Christ does not lose one of His own? The temptation is to imagine Owen preaching a serenity he could not live. The documents bear the opposite. He wrote Death of Death in 1647, before most of the burials. He wrote Communion with God in 1657, with the burials still going on around him. He began his massive Hebrews commentary in the years after, with the ejection and the small graves already settled facts behind him, and he was still at it when Mary died. The doctrine did not survive his griefs. It deepened through them.

The cross had not failed his children. The cross had carried them. The doctrine that Christ does not lose one of His own was the only doctrine that could stand at eleven graves in a row and not lie. A general atonement that "made salvation possible" would have left Owen with eleven question marks. The doctrine he actually held left him with eleven graves and one settled certainty: the One who said I shall lose none meant it, and the children given to Christ were not lost when the small bodies were lowered into the small holes. They were sent ahead.

It is worth being precise about what that cost him and what it did not. It did not spare him anything. He wept, and there is no evidence that the doctrine made him weep less; what it did was give the weeping somewhere to happen that was not the edge of a cliff. The question in those rooms narrows very fast, and by the eleventh time Owen had heard it narrow to a single syllable: did the cross do its work? He had written eight hundred pages arguing that it had. He was then asked to mean them, on a schedule not of his choosing, eleven times.

The Ship Was Never Held Up by the Rowing

On 22 August 1683, two days before he died, Owen dictated a last letter to his friend Charles Fleetwood: I am leaving the ship of the church in a storm; but while the great Pilot is in it the loss of a poor under-rower will be inconsiderable. He died at Ealing on 24 August. That morning, told that his final book on the glory of Christ had reached the press, he gave his answer: The long wished-for day is come at last, in which I shall see that glory in another manner than I have ever done or was capable of doing in this world.

The date is worth a moment. Twenty-one years earlier, to the day, the Act of Uniformity had emptied two thousand English pulpits. The twenty-fourth of August was the blackest date on the Puritan calendar, the day the institutions of England finished with men like Owen. It is the day he was fetched. The calendar the world had used to put him out was the calendar on which he was brought home.

The Pilot was in the ship. A poor under-rower could be lost without the ship being any the worse — but Owen, dictating that line two days from the end, did not expect to be lost. He had spent his life proving that the Pilot loses nobody. He was right. He has not.

The grip in that sentence was never Owen's. He did not invent it, or earn it, or keep hold of it by the quality of his theology; it had closed on him long before he could write a word about it. It is the grip of the One who said I shall lose none. Owen's comfort was never that he had believed well. It was that he had been given. If you are afraid you are holding on too weakly, you have already misread the verse: you are not what is doing the holding.

And if you are afraid of the other thing — that the none is a closed number and your name was never written into it — notice that Owen's verse has a sentence in front of it that he never separated from it. "Whoever comes to me I will never drive away." There is no screening at that door. Nobody has ever been sent back from it for arriving with the wrong credentials, or the wrong feelings, or too late. And remember how Owen got in. He did not first establish that he was among the given and then walk to Aldermanbury. He went out to hear somebody else, sat down under a stranger whose name is lost, and found out on the way home. The list is not a document anyone is handed to read. It is a thing you discover you were on, afterwards, usually while you were somewhere you had not planned to be.

Two thousand years, and He has not lost one. He is not going to begin with you.
